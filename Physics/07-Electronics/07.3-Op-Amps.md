# 7.3 Operational Amplifiers

An op-amp is a differential amplifier with absurdly high gain — typically $10^5$ to $10^6$. Alone it's useless (it saturates immediately), but with feedback resistors it becomes the most versatile building block in analog electronics. You can make it amplify, subtract, integrate, differentiate, or compare signals just by choosing where to connect the resistors.

**The Intuition:** Imagine a tug-of-war between two inputs. The op-amp's output swings wildly toward whichever side is winning, trying to make the two inputs equal. Negative feedback (connecting output back to the inverting input) acts like a rope that pulls the inputs together — it forces $V_+ = V_-$, the "virtual short" principle. This single idea makes op-amp circuits trivial to analyze.

**The Math:** The open-loop behavior is $V_{out} = A(V_+ - V_-)$ where $A \to \infty$ for an ideal op-amp. With negative feedback, the virtual short condition $V_+ = V_-$ gives us two golden rules: the non-inverting gain is $G = 1 + \frac{R_f}{R_1}$ and the inverting gain is $G = -\frac{R_f}{R_1}$. The input impedance is infinite (no current into the inputs) and output impedance is zero.

**What does this mean for Physics?** Op-amps are the bridge between analog (continuous) and digital (discrete) worlds. The differential pair at their input uses the same transistor physics as BJTs, and the virtual short principle is really a consequence of the enormous open-loop gain forcing the input difference to near-zero. Saturation at the supply rails is a reminder that real physics imposes limits.

| Config | Gain | Input impedance |
|--------|------|-----------------|
| Inverting | $-R_f/R_1$ | $R_1$ |
| Non-inverting | $1 + R_f/R_1$ | $\infty$ |
| Voltage follower | 1 | $\infty$ |

**Setup:** A non-inverting amplifier has $R_f = 90\ \text{k}\Omega$ and $R_1 = 10\ \text{k}\Omega$. Find the gain.

**Solution:** $G = 1 + \frac{90}{10} = 1 + 9 = 10$. A $0.1$ V input produces a $1$ V output. For the inverting version with the same resistors, the gain would be $-9$ (inverted and amplified).

**Key insight:** The gain depends only on the resistor ratio, not on the op-amp itself — that's the power of negative feedback. It makes circuits stable and predictable regardless of component tolerances.
