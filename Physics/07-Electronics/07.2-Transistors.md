# 7.2 Transistors

Transistors are the workhorses of modern electronics — they amplify tiny signals into big ones and switch billions of times per second inside your CPU. At their core, they're just two diodes sharing a common region, but that arrangement creates something remarkable: a voltage-controlled or current-controlled switch.

**The Intuition:** Think of a transistor as a water faucet controlled by a smaller pilot valve. A small flow (base current $I_B$ in a BJT) controls a much larger flow (collector current $I_C$). The gain $\beta$ tells you the multiplication factor — if $\beta = 100$, a $20\ \mu$A base current controls $2$ mA of collector current. FETs work similarly but use voltage instead of current: the gate voltage creates an electric field that opens or closes a conducting channel.

**The Math:** For a BJT, the fundamental relationship is $I_C = \beta I_B$, where $\beta \approx 100$ for a typical small-signal transistor. The emitter current satisfies $I_E = I_C + I_B$ (Kirchhoff's current law at the transistor level). For a JFET, the drain current follows Shockley's equation: $I_D = I_{DSS}\left(1 - \frac{V_{GS}}{V_P}\right)^2$, where $I_{DSS}$ is the drain current at zero gate-source voltage and $V_P$ is the pinch-off voltage.

**What does this mean for Physics?** Transistors exploit the quantum behavior of doped semiconductors — the depletion region width changes with applied voltage, modulating the conducting channel. The BJT's exponential $I_C$ vs $V_{BE}$ relationship is another Boltzmann distribution, while the FET's quadratic transfer curve comes from controlling the channel width electrostatically.

| Type | Terminals | Key parameter |
|------|-----------|---------------|
| NPN/PNP (BJT) | Base, Collector, Emitter | $\beta$ (current gain) |
| JFET/MOSFET | Gate, Drain, Source | $g_m$ (transconductance) |

**Setup:** A BJT with $\beta = 100$ has a base current of $I_B = 20\ \mu$A. Find the collector current.

**Solution:** $I_C = \beta I_B = 100 \times 20 \times 10^{-6} = 2\ \text{mA}$. The emitter current is $I_E = 2.02\ \text{mA}$, nearly equal to $I_C$ since $I_B \ll I_C$.

**Key insight:** The transistor is a current amplifier at its core. A microamp signal controls a milliamp load — that's the entire principle behind amplification, switching, and digital logic.
