# 5.4 Magnetic Fields

Magnetic fields exert forces on moving charges and current-carrying wires. The key difference from electric fields: magnetic force is always perpendicular to velocity, so it can change direction but not speed. The Lorentz force combines electric and magnetic effects into one expression.

**The Intuition:** Imagine a spinning top on a frictionless table. You push it sideways (electric force) to speed it up or slow it down. But magnetic force is like an invisible hand that only pushes it in circles — it changes direction without changing speed. A charged particle in a magnetic field spirals or orbits.

**The Math:**

Force on a current-carrying wire in a magnetic field:

$F = BIL\sin\theta$

Force on a single moving charge (Lorentz force):

$F = qvB\sin\theta$

Maximum force occurs when velocity is perpendicular to the field ($\theta = 90°$). The field around a long straight wire:

$B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}$

where $\mu_0 = 4\pi \times 10^{-7}$ T·m/A.

**What does this mean for Physics?**

Magnetic fields connect to [[05-Electricity-Magnetism/05.5-Electromagnetic-Induction|electromagnetic induction]] (changing $B$ creates EMF), [[05-Electricity-Magnetism/05.7-Electromagnetic-Waves|EM waves]] (oscillating $B$ fields), and particle physics (cyclotrons, mass spectrometers). The cross-product nature of magnetic force is why particle detectors use magnetic fields to identify particles by curvature radius.

---

**Setup:** A wire of length 0.5 m carries 10 A in a 0.2 T field perpendicular to the wire. Find the force.

**Solution:**

$F = BIL\sin\theta = 0.2 \times 10 \times 0.5 \times \sin 90° = 1.0\ \text{N}$

**Key insight:** The $\sin\theta$ factor means force is zero when the wire is parallel to the field. Orientation matters — magnetic force only exists when there's a component of velocity perpendicular to the field.
