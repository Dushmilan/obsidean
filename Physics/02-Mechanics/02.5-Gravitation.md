# 2.5 Gravitation

Gravity feels personal — the Earth pulls *you*. But Newton's genius was realising it's universal: every mass attracts every other mass. The same force that drops an apple also holds the Moon in orbit. Same law, vastly different scales.

**The Intuition:** Two magnets pull on each other. Gravity works similarly — except *every* bit of mass pulls on every other bit. A planet is just a huge collection of atoms all pulling together. The farther away you are, the weaker the pull — and it drops off fast: double the distance, quarter the force.

**The Math:**

| Quantity | Formula |
|----------|---------|
| Gravitational force | $F = G\frac{m_1 m_2}{r^2}$ |
| Field strength | $g = \frac{GM}{r^2}$ |
| Orbital velocity | $v_{orb} = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}$ |
| Kepler's 3rd law | $T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2}{GM}r^3$ |

where $G = 6.67 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{Nm}^2/\text{kg}^2$.

**What does this mean for Physics?** Gravitation connects [[02-Mechanics/02.4-Circular-Motion|circular motion]] to real orbits. It explains tides, satellite launches, and why galaxies hold together. It's also where [[02-Mechanics/02.3-Work-Energy-Power|energy conservation]] meets orbital mechanics — a satellite's total energy (kinetic + potential) determines its orbit shape.

---

**Setup:** Find the orbital velocity of Earth around the Sun ($M = 2 \times 10^{30}\ \text{kg}$, $r = 1.5 \times 10^{11}\ \text{m}$).

**Solution:** Set gravitational force equal to centripetal force:
$$v = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}} = \sqrt{\frac{6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times 2 \times 10^{30}}{1.5 \times 10^{11}}} = 3.0 \times 10^4\ \text{m/s}$$

**Key insight:** Earth orbits at $\approx 30\ \text{km/s}$. That's fast — but at $1.5 \times 10^{11}\ \text{m}$ away, the Sun's pull is weak enough to sustain it. Closer orbits need higher speeds.

---

**Setup:** Find the acceleration due to gravity on the surface of Mars ($M = 6.4 \times 10^{23}\ \text{kg}$, $R = 3.4 \times 10^6\ \text{m}$). Compare to Earth.

**Solution:** Using $g = \frac{GM}{R^2}$:
$$g_{Mars} = \frac{6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times 6.4 \times 10^{23}}{(3.4 \times 10^6)^2} = \frac{4.27 \times 10^{13}}{1.16 \times 10^{13}} = 3.7\ \text{m/s}^2$$

Compare to Earth: $\frac{g_{Mars}}{g_{Earth}} = \frac{3.7}{9.8} = 0.38$

Mars surface gravity is about $38\%$ of Earth's. A $100\ \text{kg}$ person would weigh $980\ \text{N}$ on Earth but only $370\ \text{N}$ on Mars.

**Key insight:** Surface gravity depends on both mass and radius. Mars is less massive but also smaller — the smaller radius partially compensates. The $1/r^2$ dependence means radius matters a lot.

---

**Setup:** A satellite orbits Earth at an altitude of $400\ \text{km}$ ($R_E = 6.4 \times 10^6\ \text{m}$, $M_E = 6 \times 10^{24}\ \text{kg}$). Find the orbital speed and period.

**Solution:** Orbital radius: $r = R_E + h = 6.4 \times 10^6 + 4 \times 10^5 = 6.8 \times 10^6\ \text{m}$.

Orbital speed:
$$v = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}} = \sqrt{\frac{6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times 6 \times 10^{24}}{6.8 \times 10^6}} = \sqrt{5.88 \times 10^7} = 7.67 \times 10^3\ \text{m/s} \approx 7.67\ \text{km/s}$$

Period:
$$T = \frac{2\pi r}{v} = \frac{2\pi \times 6.8 \times 10^6}{7.67 \times 10^3} = 5560\ \text{s} \approx 92.7\ \text{min}$$

**Key insight:** Always use $r = R + h$, not just $h$, for orbital calculations. The ISS orbits at about $400\ \text{km}$ — low enough that there's still slight atmospheric drag, requiring periodic reboosts.

## Common Mistakes

- ❌ Using the wrong radius — orbital radius is $R + h$ (surface radius + altitude), not just altitude $h$
- ❌ Forgetting that $G = 6.67 \times 10^{-11}$ is very small — gravitational effects are weak unless masses are enormous
- ❌ Confusing weight ($mg$) with gravitational force ($GMm/r^2$) — they agree near Earth's surface but are conceptually different
- ❌ Forgetting that orbital speed *decreases* with distance — farther orbits are slower
