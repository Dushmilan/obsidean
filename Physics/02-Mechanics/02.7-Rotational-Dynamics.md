# 2.7 Rotational Dynamics

Linear dynamics has $F = ma$. Rotation has its twin: $\tau = I\alpha$. Torque replaces force, moment of inertia replaces mass, and angular acceleration replaces linear acceleration. The entire framework of Newton translates to rotating systems.

**The Intuition:** A door. Push near the hinge (small $r$) — it barely moves. Push near the handle (large $r$) — it swings easily. That's torque: $\tau = rF\sin\theta$. The door's resistance to spinning depends on how its mass is distributed — that's the moment of inertia $I$. A figure skater pulls their arms in to spin faster: same angular momentum, smaller $I$, bigger $\omega$.

**The Math:**

| Quantity | Formula |
|----------|---------|
| Torque | $\tau = rF\sin\theta$ |
| Newton's 2nd (rotation) | $\tau = I\alpha$ |
| Moment of inertia | $I = \sum m_i r_i^2$ |
| Angular momentum | $L = I\omega$ |
| Rotational KE | $KE_{rot} = \frac{1}{2}I\omega^2$ |
| Parallel axis theorem | $I = I_{cm} + md^2$ |

**What does this mean for Physics?** Rotation is unavoidable. Planets spin, gears turn, electrons have angular momentum. [[02-Mechanics/02.4-Circular-Motion|Circular motion]] is the simple case; [[02-Mechanics/02.3-Work-Energy-Power|energy conservation]] with rotation (rolling, flywheels) is the practical extension. Angular momentum conservation explains neutron stars, figure skaters, and gyroscopes.

---

**Setup:** A disc ($I = \frac{1}{2}MR^2$) of mass $2\ \text{kg}$, radius $0.5\ \text{m}$, has $10\ \text{N·m}$ torque applied. Find angular acceleration.

**Solution:** First compute $I$, then apply $\tau = I\alpha$:
$$I = \frac{1}{2}(2)(0.5)^2 = 0.25\ \text{kg·m}^2$$
$$\alpha = \frac{\tau}{I} = \frac{10}{0.25} = 40\ \text{rad/s}^2$$

**Key insight:** Moment of inertia is shape-dependent. A ring ($I = MR^2$) resists rotation more than a disc ($I = \frac{1}{2}MR^2$) of the same mass — the ring's mass is farther from the axis.

---

**Setup:** A solid sphere ($I = \frac{2}{5}MR^2$) of mass $4\ \text{kg}$, radius $0.1\ \text{m}$, rolls down a $30°$ incline from rest. Find the acceleration of its centre of mass.

**Solution:** Forces along the incline (gravity component and friction):
$$mg\sin\theta - f = ma$$

Torque about centre of mass (friction provides torque):
$$\tau = fR = I\alpha = \frac{2}{5}MR^2 \cdot \frac{a}{R} = \frac{2}{5}MRa$$
$$f = \frac{2}{5}Ma$$

Substitute into force equation:
$$mg\sin\theta - \frac{2}{5}Ma = Ma$$
$$mg\sin\theta = \frac{7}{5}Ma$$
$$a = \frac{5}{7}g\sin\theta = \frac{5}{7}(10)(0.5) = 3.57\ \text{m/s}^2$$

**Key insight:** A rolling object accelerates slower than a sliding one ($g\sin\theta = 5\ \text{m/s}^2$) because some gravitational energy goes into rotational kinetic energy. The fraction depends on shape — a hollow sphere accelerates slowest, a ring slowest of all.

---

**Setup:** A skater ($I_1 = 4.0\ \text{kg·m}^2$) spins at $2\ \text{rev/s}$. She pulls her arms in and her moment of inertia drops to $2.5\ \text{kg·m}^2$. Find her new angular velocity and the work done.

**Solution:** Convert to rad/s: $\omega_1 = 2 \times 2\pi = 4\pi\ \text{rad/s} = 12.57\ \text{rad/s}$

Conservation of angular momentum: $I_1\omega_1 = I_2\omega_2$
$$\omega_2 = \frac{I_1\omega_1}{I_2} = \frac{4.0 \times 12.57}{2.5} = 20.1\ \text{rad/s} = 3.2\ \text{rev/s}$$

Work done = change in rotational KE:
$$W = \frac{1}{2}I_2\omega_2^2 - \frac{1}{2}I_1\omega_1^2$$
$$W = \frac{1}{2}(2.5)(20.1)^2 - \frac{1}{2}(4.0)(12.57)^2$$
$$W = 505 - 317.5 = 187.5\ \text{J}$$

**Key insight:** The skater does work by pulling her arms in (muscle force over a distance). This work increases her rotational KE even though angular momentum is conserved. Same $L$, but energy goes up.

## Common Mistakes

- ❌ Forgetting to use the correct $I$ for the actual shape (disc $\frac{1}{2}MR^2$ vs ring $MR^2$ vs sphere $\frac{2}{5}MR^2$)
- ❌ Mixing up rotational and linear KE when an object rolls — total KE $= \frac{1}{2}mv^2 + \frac{1}{2}I\omega^2$
- ❌ Forgetting $v = r\omega$ for rolling without slipping — this constraint links linear and angular quantities
- ❌ Applying $\tau = I\alpha$ about the wrong axis — always specify the axis clearly
