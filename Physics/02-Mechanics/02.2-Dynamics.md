# 2.2 Dynamics

If kinematics describes *how* things move, dynamics explains *why*. What makes a ball rise? What slows a car down? Forces. Dynamics is Newton's answer to the question every kid asks: "What pushed it?"

**The Intuition:** Picture a shopping cart. You push it — it accelerates. You stop pushing — it keeps going (until friction wins). A friend pushes back from the other side — it stays still. This is Newton's three laws in plain sight: inertia, $F = ma$, and action-reaction pairs.

**The Math:**

| Law | Statement |
|-----|-----------|
| 1st | $F_{net} = 0 \implies a = 0$ (equilibrium) |
| 2nd | $F = ma$ — force equals mass times acceleration |
| 3rd | $F_{AB} = -F_{BA}$ — forces come in equal, opposite pairs |

Momentum connects force and time: $p = mv$, and $F = \frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t}$.

**What does this mean for Physics?** Dynamics is the engine of mechanics. [[02-Mechanics/02.3-Work-Energy-Power|Work-energy]] gives you a scalar alternative. [[02-Mechanics/02.4-Circular-Motion|Circular motion]] uses centripetal force. [[02-Mechanics/02.7-Rotational-Dynamics|Rotation]] extends $F = ma$ to $\tau = I\alpha$. Everything flows from here.

---

**Setup:** A $5\ \text{kg}$ block on a frictionless surface is pulled by $20\ \text{N}$. Find acceleration.

**Solution:** Apply $F = ma$ directly:
$$a = \frac{F}{m} = \frac{20}{5} = 4\ \text{m/s}^2$$

**Key insight:** Mass resists acceleration. Double the mass, halve the acceleration — same force, different outcome. This is inertia made quantitative.

---

**Setup:** Two masses ($3\ \text{kg}$ and $5\ \text{kg}$) are connected by a light string over a frictionless pulley. Find the acceleration of the system and the tension in the string.

**Solution:** The heavier mass accelerates downward. Let $a$ be the acceleration and $T$ the tension.

For the $5\ \text{kg}$ mass (downward positive):
$$5g - T = 5a$$

For the $3\ \text{kg}$ mass (upward positive):
$$T - 3g = 3a$$

Add the two equations:
$$5g - 3g = 8a \implies a = \frac{2g}{8} = \frac{20}{8} = 2.5\ \text{m/s}^2$$

Substitute back for tension:
$$T = 3(g + a) = 3(10 + 2.5) = 37.5\ \text{N}$$

**Key insight:** The string tension is the same on both sides (light string, frictionless pulley). The net driving force is the weight difference, and it accelerates the total mass.

---

**Setup:** A $10\ \text{kg}$ block on a rough surface ($\mu = 0.3$) is pulled by a $50\ \text{N}$ force at $30°$ above the horizontal. Find the acceleration.

**Solution:** Resolve the applied force into components:
$$F_x = 50\cos 30° = 43.3\ \text{N}, \quad F_y = 50\sin 30° = 25\ \text{N}$$

Normal reaction (vertical equilibrium):
$$N + F_y = mg \implies N = mg - F_y = 100 - 25 = 75\ \text{N}$$

Friction:
$$f = \mu N = 0.3 \times 75 = 22.5\ \text{N}$$

Horizontal net force:
$$F_{net} = F_x - f = 43.3 - 22.5 = 20.8\ \text{N}$$

Acceleration:
$$a = \frac{F_{net}}{m} = \frac{20.8}{10} = 2.08\ \text{m/s}^2$$

**Key insight:** When a force is applied at an angle, it has two effects — it contributes to horizontal motion AND reduces the normal force (reducing friction). Always resolve forces before applying $F = ma$.

## Common Mistakes

- ❌ Forgetting to include the mass of the pulley in the system (only for massive pulleys)
- ❌ Mixing up action-reaction pairs — they act on *different* bodies, not the same body
- ❌ Forgetting the weight component along an inclined plane ($mg\sin\theta$)
- ❌ Using $F = ma$ without including friction as a force in the net force
