# 2.6 Simple Harmonic Motion

Oscillations are everywhere: a swinging pendulum, a vibrating guitar string, a car bouncing on springs. SHM is the simplest model of oscillation — the restoring force is proportional to displacement. Pull twice as far, it pushes back twice as hard.

**The Intuition:** A mass on a spring. Pull it down — it springs back. Overshoot — it pulls back the other way. The force is always proportional to displacement ($F = -kx$), and always points toward equilibrium. The result: the mass bounces back and forth forever (in an ideal world). The same math describes a pendulum for small angles.

**The Math:**

| Quantity | Formula |
|----------|---------|
| Displacement | $x = A\cos(\omega t + \phi)$ |
| Velocity | $v = \pm\omega\sqrt{A^2 - x^2}$ |
| Acceleration | $a = -\omega^2 x$ |
| Period (pendulum) | $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{l}{g}}$ |
| Period (spring) | $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}}$ |
| Total energy | $E = \frac{1}{2}kA^2$ |

where $\omega = 2\pi f = \frac{2\pi}{T}$ is angular frequency.

**What does this mean for Physics?** SHM is the foundation of [[04-Waves-Optics/04.1-Wave-Motion|wave physics]]. Every wave is SHM rippling through a medium. It also connects to [[02-Mechanics/02.4-Circular-Motion|circular motion]] — SHM is just circular motion viewed edge-on. And [[02-Mechanics/02.7-Rotational-Dynamics|rotational systems]] can oscillate too.

---

**Setup:** A spring ($k = 100\ \text{N/m}$, $m = 1\ \text{kg}$) oscillates with amplitude $0.1\ \text{m}$. Find max velocity.

**Solution:** $v_{max} = \omega A$, where $\omega = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}$:
$$v_{max} = \sqrt{\frac{100}{1}} \times 0.1 = 1.0\ \text{m/s}$$

**Key insight:** Max velocity happens at equilibrium ($x = 0$), not at maximum displacement. At the extremes, all energy is potential; at the centre, all energy is kinetic.

---

**Setup:** A pendulum of length $0.5\ \text{m}$ swings with amplitude $5°$. Find the period and maximum velocity. ($g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$)

**Solution:** Period:
$$T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{l}{g}} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{0.5}{9.8}} = 2\pi \times 0.226 = 1.42\ \text{s}$$

Angular frequency: $\omega = \frac{2\pi}{T} = \frac{2\pi}{1.42} = 4.43\ \text{rad/s}$

Amplitude in radians: $A = 5° \times \frac{\pi}{180} = 0.0873\ \text{rad}$

Arc length amplitude: $s_{max} = lA = 0.5 \times 0.0873 = 0.0436\ \text{m}$

Maximum velocity:
$$v_{max} = \omega \cdot s_{max} = 4.43 \times 0.0436 = 0.193\ \text{m/s}$$

**Key insight:** The pendulum period depends only on length and gravity — not on mass or amplitude (for small angles). This is why pendulums were used in clocks: their period is remarkably stable.

---

**Setup:** A mass-spring system ($m = 0.2\ \text{kg}$, $k = 80\ \text{N/m}$) is displaced $5\ \text{cm}$ and released. Find the velocity when the displacement is $2\ \text{cm}$.

**Solution:** Angular frequency: $\omega = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{80}{0.2}} = 20\ \text{rad/s}$

Using $v = \omega\sqrt{A^2 - x^2}$:
$$v = 20\sqrt{(0.05)^2 - (0.02)^2} = 20\sqrt{0.0025 - 0.0004} = 20\sqrt{0.0021}$$
$$v = 20 \times 0.0458 = 0.917\ \text{m/s}$$

**Key insight:** Energy conservation gives the same result: $\frac{1}{2}kA^2 = \frac{1}{2}kx^2 + \frac{1}{2}mv^2$. The velocity increases as you approach equilibrium because potential energy converts to kinetic energy.

## Common Mistakes

- ❌ Confusing frequency and period — $f = \frac{1}{T}$, not $T = \frac{1}{f}$
- ❌ Using $\sin$ instead of $\cos$ for displacement — both work but must be consistent throughout
- ❌ Forgetting SHM only applies for small angles in a pendulum (typically $< 10°$)
- ❌ Mixing up amplitude and maximum displacement from equilibrium — they're the same thing in SHM
