# 2.8 Fluid Dynamics

Liquids and gases flow — and flowing fluids obey surprising rules. A wider pipe means slower flow. A faster stream means lower pressure. These aren't obvious, but they follow directly from conservation laws: mass, energy, and pressure.

**The Intuition:** Put your hand out of a car window, palm facing the wind. Tilt it slightly — your hand rises. The air moves faster over the curved top, creating lower pressure there. Higher pressure below pushes your hand up. That's Bernoulli's principle in action. A garden hose does the same: pinch the nozzle, water speeds up and pressure drops.

**The Math:**

| Principle | Formula |
|-----------|---------|
| Pressure | $p = \frac{F}{A}$ |
| Hydrostatic pressure | $p = p_0 + \rho gh$ |
| Buoyancy (Archimedes) | $F_B = \rho V g$ |
| Bernoulli's equation | $p + \frac{1}{2}\rho v^2 + \rho gh = \text{const}$ |
| Continuity equation | $A_1 v_1 = A_2 v_2$ |

Continuity says what goes in must come out — mass is conserved. Bernoulli says as speed increases, pressure drops (at constant height).

**What does this mean for Physics?** Fluid dynamics bridges [[02-Mechanics/02.2-Dynamics|dynamics]] and [[03-Thermal-Physics/03.2-Gas-Laws|thermodynamics]]. It explains airplane lift, blood flow, weather systems, and hydraulic machines. The analogy to [[05-Electricity-Magnetism/05.3-Current-Electricity|electrical circuits]] (current = flow, voltage = pressure) makes it conceptually powerful.

---

**Setup:** Water flows through a pipe that narrows from $10\ \text{cm}^2$ to $5\ \text{cm}^2$. Velocity in the wide section is $2\ \text{m/s}$. Find velocity in the narrow section.

**Solution:** Apply the continuity equation $A_1 v_1 = A_2 v_2$:
$$v_2 = \frac{A_1 v_1}{A_2} = \frac{10 \times 2}{5} = 4\ \text{m/s}$$

**Key insight:** Half the area, double the velocity. The flow rate ($A \cdot v$) stays constant. Narrowing a pipe always speeds up the fluid — regardless of what the fluid is.

---

**Setup:** Water flows through a horizontal pipe at $0.02\ \text{m}^3/\text{s}$. The wide section has area $0.01\ \text{m}^2$ and the narrow section $0.005\ \text{m}^2$. Find the pressure difference between the two sections. ($\rho = 1000\ \text{kg/m}^3$)

**Solution:** Find velocities from continuity:
$$v_1 = \frac{Q}{A_1} = \frac{0.02}{0.01} = 2\ \text{m/s}$$
$$v_2 = \frac{Q}{A_2} = \frac{0.02}{0.005} = 4\ \text{m/s}$$

Apply Bernoulli's equation at the same height ($h_1 = h_2$):
$$p_1 + \frac{1}{2}\rho v_1^2 = p_2 + \frac{1}{2}\rho v_2^2$$
$$p_1 - p_2 = \frac{1}{2}\rho(v_2^2 - v_1^2) = \frac{1}{2}(1000)(16 - 4) = 6000\ \text{Pa}$$

**Key insight:** The pressure drops by $6\ \text{kPa}$ in the narrow section. Faster fluid means lower pressure — this is exactly how a venturi meter works and how airplane wings generate lift.

---

**Setup:** A wooden block (density $600\ \text{kg/m}^3$) floats in water (density $1000\ \text{kg/m}^3$). What fraction of the block is submerged?

**Solution:** At equilibrium, weight = buoyant force:
$$mg = \rho_{water} V_{submerged} g$$
$$\rho_{block} V_{total} = \rho_{water} V_{submerged}$$
$$\frac{V_{submerged}}{V_{total}} = \frac{\rho_{block}}{\rho_{water}} = \frac{600}{1000} = 0.6$$

$60\%$ of the block is submerged. The remaining $40\%$ is above the waterline.

**Key insight:** The submerged fraction equals the ratio of densities — independent of the block's size or shape. This is why ice ($917\ \text{kg/m}^3$) floats with about $91.7\%$ submerged in water, and why huge icebergs are mostly underwater.

## Common Mistakes

- ❌ Using Bernoulli's equation when height changes significantly — include the $\rho gh$ terms or use hydrostatic pressure separately
- ❌ Confusing gauge pressure ($p = \rho gh$) with absolute pressure ($p = p_0 + \rho gh$)
- ❌ Forgetting the continuity equation assumes an incompressible fluid — it doesn't apply to gases at high speed
- ❌ Mixing up viscosity (resistance to flow) with density (mass per volume) — they're different properties
