# 2.4 Circular Motion

Straight-line motion is simple. Circular motion is where things get interesting — even at constant speed, the velocity vector *changes direction*, which means acceleration exists. That acceleration points inward, toward the centre.

**The Intuition:** Spin a ball on a string. The ball wants to fly off in a straight line (inertia), but the string pulls it inward. The tug-of-war between the ball's inertia and the string's tension produces circular motion. If the string snaps, the ball flies off tangentially — not outward, not inward, but *along the tangent*.

**The Math:**

| Quantity | Formula |
|----------|---------|
| Centripetal acceleration | $a_c = \frac{v^2}{r}$ |
| Centripetal force | $F_c = \frac{mv^2}{r}$ |
| Linear-angular link | $v = r\omega$ |
| Period | $T = \frac{2\pi r}{v}$ |

Centripetal force is not a new force — it's the *net force* toward the centre. Tension, gravity, friction, or normal force can all provide it.

**What does this mean for Physics?** Circular motion is the bridge to [[02-Mechanics/02.5-Gravitation|gravitational orbits]], [[02-Mechanics/02.6-SHM|simple harmonic motion]] (SHM is projected circular motion), and [[02-Mechanics/02.7-Rotational-Dynamics|rotation]]. It's everywhere: planets, turbines, banked roads, particle accelerators.

---

**Setup:** A $0.5\ \text{kg}$ ball on a $1\ \text{m}$ string swings at $3\ \text{m/s}$. Find the tension.

**Solution:** Tension provides centripetal force:
$$T = \frac{mv^2}{r} = \frac{0.5 \times 9}{1} = 4.5\ \text{N}$$

**Key insight:** If you swing faster ($v$ increases), tension rises quadratically. At some speed, the string snaps — that's the breaking point. Engineers design for this.

---

**Setup:** A car of mass $1200\ \text{kg}$ rounds a bend of radius $50\ \text{m}$ at $20\ \text{m/s}$. Find the friction force required. What happens if $\mu = 0.4$?

**Solution:** Friction provides the centripetal force:
$$f = \frac{mv^2}{r} = \frac{1200 \times 400}{50} = 9600\ \text{N}$$

Maximum available friction:
$$f_{max} = \mu N = \mu mg = 0.4 \times 1200 \times 10 = 4800\ \text{N}$$

Since $f_{required} = 9600\ \text{N} > f_{max} = 4800\ \text{N}$, the car skids. The friction isn't enough to provide the centripetal force at this speed and radius.

Maximum safe speed:
$$v_{max} = \sqrt{\frac{\mu gr}{1}} = \sqrt{0.4 \times 10 \times 50} = \sqrt{200} = 14.1\ \text{m/s}$$

**Key insight:** The car needs $9600\ \text{N}$ of friction but can only get $4800\ \text{N}$. It slides outward. This is why roads are banked — the normal force contributes a horizontal component, reducing the friction demand.

---

**Setup:** A $0.5\ \text{kg}$ ball swings in a vertical circle of radius $1\ \text{m}$. Find the minimum speed at the top for the string to stay taut.

**Solution:** At the top, both tension and weight point downward (toward centre). The minimum condition is $T = 0$ (string just barely taut):
$$mg = \frac{mv_{min}^2}{r} \implies v_{min} = \sqrt{gr} = \sqrt{10 \times 1} = 3.16\ \text{m/s}$$

At the bottom, by energy conservation:
$$\frac{1}{2}mv_{bottom}^2 = \frac{1}{2}mv_{top}^2 + mg(2r)$$
$$v_{bottom}^2 = v_{top}^2 + 4gr = 10 + 40 = 50 \implies v_{bottom} = 7.07\ \text{m/s}$$

**Key insight:** In a vertical circle, the minimum speed at the top is $\sqrt{gr}$, not zero. If the ball goes slower, it falls out of the circular path — the string goes slack. Speed varies around the circle: fastest at the bottom, slowest at the top.

## Common Mistakes

- ❌ Confusing centripetal force with a new type of force — it's always the *net* force toward the centre, provided by existing forces
- ❌ Forgetting centripetal acceleration points *inward*, not outward (centrifugal force is a fictitious force in a rotating frame)
- ❌ Using $v = r\omega$ incorrectly — $\omega$ must be in rad/s, not rev/s or rev/min
- ❌ Assuming the minimum speed at the top of a vertical circle is $0$ — it's $\sqrt{gr}$
