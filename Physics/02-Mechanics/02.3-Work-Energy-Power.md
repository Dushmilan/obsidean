# 2.3 Work, Energy & Power

Forces are vectors — they have direction, and that complicates things. Energy gives you a scalar shortcut. Instead of tracking every force along a path, you just compare the start and end states. Work is the bridge between force and energy.

**The Intuition:** Think of work as effort. Push a box across a room — you did work. Push against a wall and nothing moves — zero work. The key is the *component of force in the direction of motion*. Energy is like money: it can change accounts (kinetic to potential, potential to kinetic) but the total stays constant if no one takes a cut (no friction, no air resistance).

**The Math:**

| Concept | Formula |
|---------|---------|
| Work | $W = Fs\cos\theta$ |
| Kinetic energy | $KE = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$ |
| Potential energy | $PE = mgh$ |
| Power | $P = \frac{W}{t}$ or $P = Fv$ |

The work-energy theorem ties it all together: $W_{net} = \Delta KE$. A net force over a distance changes the kinetic energy — period.

**What does this mean for Physics?** Energy conservation is arguably the most powerful idea in all of physics. It lets you solve [[02-Mechanics/02.6-SHM|oscillation]], [[02-Mechanics/02.4-Circular-Motion|orbital]], and [[02-Mechanics/02.5-Gravitation|gravitational]] problems without integrating forces. It extends to [[03-Thermal-Physics/03.3-First-Law|thermodynamics]] and beyond.

---

**Setup:** A $2\ \text{kg}$ ball falls from $10\ \text{m}$. Find speed at the ground (no air resistance).

**Solution:** Conservation of energy: $PE_{top} = KE_{bottom}$:
$$mgh = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 \implies v = \sqrt{2gh} = \sqrt{2 \times 10 \times 10} = 14.1\ \text{m/s}$$

**Key insight:** The mass cancels. A feather and a bowling ball hit the ground at the same speed — Galileo was right. Only height and gravity matter.

---

**Setup:** A $2\ \text{kg}$ projectile is launched at $20\ \text{m/s}$ at $45°$. Find the kinetic energy at the top of the trajectory and the potential energy at maximum height.

**Solution:** At max height, vertical velocity is zero. Only horizontal velocity remains:
$$v_x = 20\cos 45° = 14.14\ \text{m/s}$$

Kinetic energy at top:
$$KE_{top} = \frac{1}{2}mv_x^2 = \frac{1}{2}(2)(14.14)^2 = 200\ \text{J}$$

By energy conservation: $KE_{initial} = KE_{top} + PE_{top}$
$$KE_{initial} = \frac{1}{2}(2)(20)^2 = 400\ \text{J}$$
$$PE_{top} = 400 - 200 = 200\ \text{J}$$

Check: $PE = mgh \implies h = \frac{PE}{mg} = \frac{200}{20} = 10\ \text{m}$

**Key insight:** At the top of a projectile trajectory, the object still has kinetic energy — it's still moving horizontally. Energy is never zero at the peak unless the object was launched straight up.

---

**Setup:** A car of mass $1000\ \text{kg}$ accelerates from $0$ to $20\ \text{m/s}$ in $10\ \text{s}$. Find the average power delivered by the engine.

**Solution:** Work done = change in kinetic energy:
$$W = \Delta KE = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 - 0 = \frac{1}{2}(1000)(20)^2 = 200{,}000\ \text{J}$$

Average power:
$$P_{avg} = \frac{W}{t} = \frac{200{,}000}{10} = 20{,}000\ \text{W} = 20\ \text{kW}$$

**Key insight:** Average power depends only on total energy transfer and time — not on how the acceleration varies during that time. Instantaneous power ($P = Fv$) changes moment to moment, but average power is just total work over total time.

---

## Common Mistakes

- ❌ Using $W = Fs$ when force and displacement aren't parallel — always include $\cos\theta$
- ❌ Confusing weight ($mg$) with mass ($m$) — they have different units and meanings
- ❌ Forgetting that energy conservation only holds when no non-conservative forces (like friction) do work
- ❌ Mixing up elastic and inelastic collisions — kinetic energy is conserved only in elastic ones
