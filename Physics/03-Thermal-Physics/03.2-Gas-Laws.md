# 3.2 Gas Laws

Imagine a box full of bouncing balls. If you squeeze the box, the balls hit the walls harder — that's pressure increasing. Heat the box, the balls move faster — pressure and volume both respond. The ideal gas law captures this elegant dance between pressure, volume, temperature, and the amount of gas.

**The Intuition:** A gas is just a swarm of tiny particles bouncing off walls and each other. The pressure you feel is millions of collisions per second. Temperature controls how fast they move, volume controls how much space they have, and the amount of gas controls how many particles are in play.

**The Math:** The master equation is $pV = nRT$, where $p$ is pressure, $V$ is volume, $n$ is moles of gas, $R = 8.31$ J/(mol·K) is the gas constant, and $T$ is absolute temperature. For comparing two states: $\frac{p_1 V_1}{T_1} = \frac{p_2 V_2}{T_2}$. Kinetic theory links microscopic and macroscopic: $pV = \frac{1}{3}Nm\overline{c^2}$, and mean kinetic energy per molecule is $\overline{KE} = \frac{3}{2}kT$ where $k = 1.38 \times 10^{-23}$ J/K.

**What does this mean for Physics?** The ideal gas law connects [[03-Thermal-Physics/03.1-Temperature-Heat|temperature]] to mechanical quantities you can measure — pressure gauges and volume measurements become thermometers. Kinetic theory bridges the gap between microscopic molecular motion and macroscopic thermodynamics, which is how [[03-Thermal-Physics/03.3-First-Law|internal energy]] gets its physical meaning.

---

**Setup:** 2 mol of gas at 300 K occupies 0.05 m³. Find the pressure.

**Solution:** $p = \frac{nRT}{V} = \frac{2 \times 8.31 \times 300}{0.05} = 99{,}720$ Pa $\approx 100$ kPa.

**Key insight:** That's roughly atmospheric pressure — room-temperature gas at reasonable density behaves like the air around you. The ideal gas law is accurate at low pressure and high temperature where molecules are far apart and interactions are negligible.

---

**Setup:** A gas at 300 K and 2 atm is compressed isothermally to half its original volume. Find the final pressure. Then if heated at constant volume to 600 K, find the final pressure.

**Solution:**
- Isothermal compression ($T = 300$ K): $p_1 V_1 = p_2 V_2 \Rightarrow p_2 = p_1 \frac{V_1}{V_2} = 2 \times 2 = 4$ atm.
- Constant volume heating ($V$ fixed): $\frac{p_2}{T_2} = \frac{p_3}{T_3} \Rightarrow p_3 = 4 \times \frac{600}{300} = 8$ atm.

**Key insight:** Doubling pressure from isothermal compression, then doubling again from heating at constant volume — pressure scales proportionally with temperature when volume is held fixed. This is why a car tire feels firmer after a long drive.

---

**Setup:** Find the rms speed of nitrogen molecules ($M = 0.028$ kg/mol) at 300 K. What temperature gives double this speed? ($R = 8.31$ J/(mol·K))

**Solution:**
- $v_{rms} = \sqrt{\frac{3RT}{M}} = \sqrt{\frac{3 \times 8.31 \times 300}{0.028}} = \sqrt{267{,}321} \approx 517$ m/s.
- For double speed: $v = 1034$ m/s. Since $v \propto \sqrt{T}$, doubling speed requires quadrupling $T$: $T = 4 \times 300 = 1200$ K.

**Key insight:** Speed scales with $\sqrt{T}$, not $T$ — you need four times the absolute temperature to double the molecular speed. This is why rocket exhausts need extreme temperatures to achieve high exhaust velocities.

## Common Mistakes

- ❌ Forgetting to convert temperature to Kelvin
- ❌ Using wrong value of $R$ (8.31 for J/(mol·K) vs 0.0821 for L·atm/(mol·K))
- ❌ Confusing moles ($n$) with molecules ($N$)
- ❌ Using ideal gas law for real gases at high pressure or low temperature
