# 3.6 Kinetic Theory of Gases

Kinetic theory of gases bridges the microscopic world of molecular motion with the macroscopic laws of thermodynamics. It explains how the chaotic, high-speed collisions of trillions of molecules give rise to observable quantities like pressure and temperature. Rather than tracking individual molecules, kinetic theory uses statistical reasoning — averaging over huge numbers of particles — to connect Newtonian mechanics to the ideal gas law.

**The Intuition:** Think of a box filled with tiny bouncing balls. Every molecule is a ball, zipping around in straight lines until it slams into a wall or another molecule. Temperature controls how fast these balls move — higher temperature means faster molecules. Pressure is the cumulative effect of millions of collisions per second on the container walls. Each collision transfers momentum, and the average momentum transfer per collision multiplied by the collision rate gives pressure. The beauty is that you don't need to know any single molecule's path — only the statistical distribution of speeds across the ensemble.

**The Math:**

| Quantity | Formula |
|----------|---------|
| Ideal gas law (microscopic) | $pV = \frac{1}{3}Nm\overline{c^2}$ |
| Mean KE per molecule | $\overline{KE} = \frac{3}{2}kT$ |
| RMS speed | $c_{rms} = \sqrt{\frac{3kT}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{3RT}{M}}$ |
| Mean speed | $\bar{c} = \sqrt{\frac{8kT}{\pi m}}$ |
| Most probable speed | $c_{mp} = \sqrt{\frac{2kT}{m}}$ |
| Degrees of freedom (monatomic) | $f = 3$ |
| Degrees of freedom (diatomic) | $f = 5$ (at room temp) |
| Cv (molar) | $C_V = \frac{f}{2}R$ |
| Cp (molar) | $C_P = C_V + R = \frac{f+2}{2}R$ |
| Adiabatic index | $\gamma = \frac{C_P}{C_V} = \frac{f+2}{f}$ |

Where k = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J/K is Boltzmann's constant, M is molar mass (kg/mol), N is number of molecules, and m is mass per molecule (kg).

**What does this mean for Physics?** Kinetic theory explains *why* the ideal gas law works — it derives pV = nRT from first principles by applying Newton's laws to molecular collisions. It also explains heat capacity: monatomic gases (He, Ne) have Cv = 3/2 R because they possess 3 translational degrees of freedom (motion along x, y, z). Diatomic gases (N₂, O₂) have Cv = 5/2 R at room temperature because they gain 2 additional rotational degrees of freedom. This is the bridge between [[03-Thermal-Physics/03.2-Gas-Laws|microscopic molecular motion]] and [[03-Thermal-Physics/03.1-Temperature-Heat|macroscopic temperature]].

**Setup:** Find the rms speed of oxygen molecules (M = 0.032 kg/mol) at 300 K. (R = 8.31 J/(mol·K))

**Solution:**
$$c_{rms} = \sqrt{\frac{3RT}{M}} = \sqrt{\frac{3 \times 8.31 \times 300}{0.032}} = \sqrt{\frac{7479}{0.032}} = \sqrt{233719} \approx 483 \text{ m/s}$$

**Key insight:** This is about 1.4 times the speed of sound — which makes sense, since sound propagation depends on molecular collisions. Oxygen molecules at room temperature zip around at nearly half a kilometre per second.

---

**Setup:** A monatomic ideal gas (Cv = 3/2 R) at 300 K is compressed adiabatically from 0.05 m³ to 0.02 m³. Find the final temperature. (γ = 5/3)

**Solution:** For an adiabatic process: $TV^{\gamma-1} = \text{const}$
$$T_1 V_1^{\gamma-1} = T_2 V_2^{\gamma-1}$$
$$300 \times (0.05)^{2/3} = T_2 \times (0.02)^{2/3}$$
$$T_2 = 300 \times \left(\frac{0.05}{0.02}\right)^{2/3} = 300 \times (2.5)^{2/3} \approx 300 \times 1.84 = 552 \text{ K}$$

**Key insight:** The gas heats up during adiabatic compression because work is done on it and no heat escapes. The exponent γ determines how much — monatomic gases (γ = 5/3) heat up less than diatomic gases (γ = 7/5) for the same compression ratio.

---

**Setup:** A gas engine (Carnot) operates between 600 K and 300 K. If the gas is monatomic (γ = 5/3), find the efficiency. What would the efficiency be if the gas were diatomic (γ = 7/5)?

**Solution:** For a Carnot engine, efficiency depends only on temperatures:
$$e = 1 - \frac{T_C}{T_H} = 1 - \frac{300}{600} = 0.5 = 50\%$$

This is the same regardless of the gas type — Carnot efficiency depends only on the reservoir temperatures, not on γ. However, for a real Otto cycle engine (like a car engine), the efficiency is:
$$e_{Otto} = 1 - \frac{1}{r^{\gamma-1}}$$
where r is the compression ratio. For r = 8:
- Monatomic: $e = 1 - 1/8^{2/3} = 1 - 1/4 = 0.75 = 75\%$
- Diatomic: $e = 1 - 1/8^{2/5} = 1 - 1/3.03 = 0.67 = 67\%$

**Key insight:** Real engines are not Carnot cycles, so γ matters. Diatomic gases (like air) have lower γ, giving lower theoretical efficiency for the same compression ratio. This is one reason why increasing compression ratio improves fuel efficiency — up to the point of knocking.

## Common Mistakes

- ❌ Using $c_{rms} = \sqrt{3kT/m}$ with molar mass M instead of molecular mass m (or vice versa) — check units: kT uses m (kg per molecule), RT uses M (kg per mole)
- ❌ Forgetting that degrees of freedom change with temperature — diatomic gases have f = 3 at very low temperatures (rotation freezes out), f = 5 at room temperature, f = 7 at very high temperatures (vibration activates)
- ❌ Confusing Cv and Cp — Cv is for constant volume (all heat goes to temperature), Cp is for constant pressure (some heat goes to expansion work), so Cp > Cv always
- ❌ Using ideal gas law for real gases at high pressure/low temperature — kinetic theory assumes no intermolecular forces and point particles
- ❌ Forgetting γ = Cp/Cv — this ratio determines adiabatic process behavior and is crucial for engine calculations
