# 3.3 First Law of Thermodynamics

Energy cannot be created or destroyed — only moved around. The first law of thermodynamics says that when you add heat to a system, it either raises the system's internal energy or does work on the surroundings. There's no third option, and nothing gets lost. It's the universe's strictest accounting rule.

**The Intuition:** Think of a bank account. Heat $Q$ is a deposit, work $W$ is a withdrawal, and internal energy $U$ is your balance. The first law just says: $\Delta U = Q - W$. Your balance changes by what you put in minus what you spend. You can't spend money you never deposited.

**The Math:** The core equation is $\Delta U = Q - W$. At constant pressure, work is $W = p\Delta V$. For an isothermal expansion of an ideal gas, $W = nRT\ln\frac{V_2}{V_1}$. Different processes route energy differently:

| Process | Condition | $\Delta U$ | $Q$ | $W$ |
|---------|-----------|------------|-----|-----|
| Isothermal | $T$ const | 0 | $W$ | $nRT\ln(V_2/V_1)$ |
| Isochoric | $V$ const | $nC_v\Delta T$ | $\Delta U$ | 0 |
| Isobaric | $p$ const | $nC_v\Delta T$ | $nC_p\Delta T$ | $p\Delta V$ |

**What does this mean for Physics?** The first law constrains every energy conversion in the universe — engines, refrigerators, stars, living cells. It tells you exactly how much work you can extract from a given heat input, which sets the stage for [[03-Thermal-Physics/03.5-Second-Law|efficiency limits]]. It also explains why compressing a gas heats it up: you're doing work on it, and that energy has to go somewhere.

---

**Setup:** 2 mol of ideal gas expands isothermally at 300 K from 0.01 m³ to 0.03 m³. Find the work done.

**Solution:** $W = nRT\ln\frac{V_2}{V_1} = 2 \times 8.31 \times 300 \times \ln 3 = 5{,}480$ J.

**Key insight:** Since $\Delta U = 0$ for isothermal expansion of an ideal gas, all the heat added becomes work output. But this only works because the gas stays at constant temperature — in reality, maintaining that temperature requires a热 reservoir, which connects to [[03-Thermal-Physics/03.4-Heat-Transfer|heat transfer]] mechanisms.

---

**Setup:** 1 mol of ideal gas is heated from 300 K to 400 K at constant volume. Find $Q$, $W$, and $\Delta U$. ($C_v = 20.8$ J/(mol·K), $R = 8.31$ J/(mol·K))

**Solution:**
- $W = 0$ (constant volume, no expansion work).
- $\Delta U = nC_v\Delta T = 1 \times 20.8 \times 100 = 2{,}080$ J.
- From first law: $Q = \Delta U + W = 2{,}080 + 0 = 2{,}080$ J.

**Key insight:** At constant volume, all heat input goes directly into internal energy — none is wasted on expansion. This is why a pressure cooker heats food faster: it maintains high pressure (constant volume) so more energy goes into raising temperature rather than doing work against the atmosphere.

---

**Setup:** An ideal gas undergoes the cycle: A→B is isothermal expansion ($V$ doubles), B→C is isochoric cooling ($T$ halves), C→A is isobaric compression ($V$ halves). Find net work done per mole if $T_A = 400$ K.

**Solution:**
- A→B (isothermal): $W_{AB} = nRT_A \ln 2 = 1 \times 8.31 \times 400 \times 0.693 = 2{,}304$ J. $\Delta U_{AB} = 0$.
- B→C (isochoric): $W_{BC} = 0$. $\Delta U_{BC} = nC_v(T_C - T_B) = C_v(200 - 400)$.
- C→A (isobaric): $W_{CA} = p\Delta V$. Since $p$ constant and $V$ halves, $T$ halves too: $T_A/T_C = V_A/V_C \Rightarrow 400/200 = 2$ ✓. $W_{CA} = nR\Delta T = 1 \times 8.31 \times (400 - 200) = 1{,}662$ J.

Actually, for the full cycle: $\Delta U_{total} = 0$ (state function), so $W_{net} = Q_{net}$.

- $Q_{AB} = W_{AB} = 2{,}304$ J
- $Q_{BC} = \Delta U_{BC} = C_v \times (-200) = -4{,}160$ J (using $C_v = 20.8$)
- $Q_{CA} = nC_p\Delta T = 1 \times 29.1 \times 200 = 5{,}820$ J (using $C_p = C_v + R = 29.1$)

Wait — let me recalculate more carefully. For C→A isobaric compression: $\Delta T = T_A - T_C = 400 - 200 = 200$ K. $W_{CA} = p\Delta V = nR\Delta T = 1{,}662$ J (positive since gas is compressed... actually convention: $W$ by gas is positive, gas contracts so $W_{CA} = -1{,}662$ J).

$W_{net} = W_{AB} + W_{BC} + W_{CA} = 2{,}304 + 0 - 1{,}662 = 642$ J.

**Key insight:** The net work is the area enclosed by the cycle on a p-V diagram. A larger area means more work extracted per cycle, which is why engineers optimize cycle shapes in real engines.

## Common Mistakes

- ❌ Forgetting that $\Delta U = 0$ for isothermal ideal gas process
- ❌ Confusing $C_p$ and $C_v$ ($C_p = C_v + R$)
- ❌ Using $W = p\Delta V$ only for constant pressure processes
- ❌ Forgetting sign convention ($W$ by gas is positive, $W$ on gas is negative)
