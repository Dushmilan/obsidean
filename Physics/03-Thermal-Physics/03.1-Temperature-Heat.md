# 3.1 Temperature & Heat

You already know that a hot cup of tea eventually cools down. But what actually *is* temperature? It turns out temperature isn't a fundamental substance — it's a measure of how fast molecules are jiggling around on average. Heat, meanwhile, is what *flows* between two objects at different temperatures. Touch something hotter, and energy moves into your hand.

**The Intuition:** Temperature is like the "average mood" of a crowd — it tells you the typical energy of particles, not what any single particle is doing. Heat is what happens when a lively crowd bumps into a calm one: energy transfers until both sides are equally energetic.

**The Math:** The key relationship is $Q = mc\Delta T$, where $Q$ is heat energy, $m$ is mass, $c$ is specific heat capacity, and $\Delta T$ is temperature change. For phase transitions with no temperature change, it's $Q = mL$ where $L$ is the latent heat. Temperature conversion between Celsius and Fahrenheit uses $\frac{C}{F - 32} = \frac{100}{180}$.

| Scale | Freezing | Boiling | Absolute Zero |
|-------|----------|---------|---------------|
| Celsius | 0°C | 100°C | -273.15°C |
| Fahrenheit | 32°F | 212°F | -459.67°F |
| Kelvin | 0 K | 373.15 K | 0 K |

**What does this mean for Physics?** Temperature scales anchor everything in [[03-Thermal-Physics/03.2-Gas-Laws|gas law]] calculations — use Kelvin or your numbers break. The formula $Q = mc\Delta T$ shows up everywhere from engine cooling to climate models. Latent heat $Q = mL$ explains why ice stays at 0°C while melting: all the energy goes into breaking bonds, not raising temperature.

---

**Setup:** Find the heat needed to raise 0.5 kg of water from 20°C to 80°C, given $c = 4200$ J/(kg·K).

**Solution:** $Q = mc\Delta T = 0.5 \times 4200 \times 60 = 126{,}000$ J $= 126$ kJ.

**Key insight:** That's a lot of energy for a modest temperature swing — water's high specific heat capacity is why oceans moderate Earth's climate and why [[03-Thermal-Physics/03.3-First-Law|energy conservation]] matters in thermal systems.

---

**Setup:** How much energy is needed to melt 2 kg of ice at 0°C and then heat the water to 50°C? ($L_{fusion} = 334$ kJ/kg, $c_{water} = 4200$ J/(kg·K))

**Solution:**
- Step 1 (melting): $Q_1 = mL_f = 2 \times 334{,}000 = 668{,}000$ J.
- Step 2 (heating): $Q_2 = mc\Delta T = 2 \times 4200 \times 50 = 420{,}000$ J.
- Total: $Q = 668{,}000 + 420{,}000 = 1{,}088{,}000$ J $\approx 1.09$ MJ.

**Key insight:** Notice that melting the ice takes *more* energy than heating the resulting water by 50°C. Latent heat is large — phase transitions are energy-hungry processes.

---

**Setup:** A 0.5 kg copper block ($c = 385$ J/(kg·K)) at 200°C is dropped into 1 kg of water at 20°C. Find the final equilibrium temperature.

**Solution:** At equilibrium, heat lost by copper = heat gained by water:
$m_c c_c (T_{c,i} - T_f) = m_w c_w (T_f - T_{w,i})$

$0.5 \times 385 \times (200 - T_f) = 1 \times 4200 \times (T_f - 20)$

$38{,}500 - 192.5 \, T_f = 4200 \, T_f - 84{,}000$

$122{,}500 = 4392.5 \, T_f$

$T_f = \frac{122{,}500}{4392.5} \approx 27.9°\text{C}$

**Key insight:** The water barely warms up despite the copper being very hot — water's specific heat is over 10 times copper's. This is why a splash of hot water doesn't scald you but a copper pan at the same temperature would.

## Common Mistakes

- ❌ Using Celsius instead of Kelvin in gas law calculations
- ❌ Confusing specific heat capacity ($c$) with latent heat ($L$)
- ❌ Forgetting that phase change means constant temperature
- ❌ Using wrong sign for $\Delta T$ (final − initial, not |final − initial|)
