# Temperature & Heat

## Definition

- **Temperature** — a measure of the average kinetic energy of particles (K; never °C in gas laws).
- **Heat** — energy transferred due to a temperature difference (J).
- **Specific heat capacity:** $Q = mc\Delta T$ (J/(kg·K)).
- **Latent heat:** $Q = mL$ — energy for phase change with *no* temperature change.

| Scale | Freezing | Boiling | Absolute zero |
|-------|----------|---------|---------------|
| Celsius | 0 °C | 100 °C | −273.15 °C |
| Fahrenheit | 32 °F | 212 °F | −459.67 °F |
| Kelvin | 273.15 K | 373.15 K | 0 K |

## The Intuition

Temperature is the "average mood" of a crowd — typical particle energy, not any single particle. Heat is a lively crowd bumping into a calm one: energy transfers until both sides are equally energetic. Ice stays at 0 °C while melting because all the energy breaks bonds instead of raising temperature.

## The Physics

| Quantity | Equation | Units |
|----------|----------|-------|
| Heat (temperature change) | $Q = mc\Delta T$ | J |
| Heat (phase change) | $Q = mL$ | J |
| Conversion | $\frac{C}{F-32} = \frac{100}{180}$ | — |

## Derivation

Specific heat capacity is defined by $c = Q/(m\Delta T)$ — measured calorimetrically. Latent heat is the bond-breaking energy per unit mass, measured at constant temperature during the transition. [Full derivations: [[Maths/Pure/01-Algebra/03-Logarithms/03-Logarithms]]]

## Method

1. Identify the stages: heating ($Q = mc\Delta T$) and/or phase change ($Q = mL$).
2. Use Kelvin in any formula involving temperature ratios or gas laws.
3. Sum the heat for each stage.

## Worked Examples

**Setup:** Heat 0.5 kg of water from 20 °C to 80 °C ($c = 4200$ J/(kg·K)).

**Solution:** $Q = mc\Delta T = 0.5\times4200\times60 = 126$ kJ.

**Key insight:** Water's high heat capacity is why oceans moderate Earth's climate.

---

**Setup:** Melt 2 kg of ice at 0 °C then heat to 50 °C ($L_f = 334$ kJ/kg).

**Solution:** $Q_1 = mL_f = 668$ kJ; $Q_2 = mc\Delta T = 420$ kJ; total $\approx 1.09$ MJ.

**Key insight:** Melting takes *more* energy than heating the water by 50 °C — phase transitions are energy-hungry.

## Common Traps

- Using °C where Kelvin is required (gas laws, ratios)
- Confusing specific heat capacity (per kg·K) with heat capacity (per object)
- Forgetting latent heat during phase change — temperature doesn't rise there
- Sign conventions in $Q = mc\Delta T$ — use $\Delta T = T_{final} - T_{initial}$
