# 3.4 Heat Transfer

Heat doesn't just teleport from hot to cold — it travels through specific mechanisms. Conduction passes energy through molecular vibrations. Convection sweeps it along with moving fluid. Radiation sends it out as electromagnetic waves, needing no medium at all. Understanding these three pathways is how you predict how fast things heat up or cool down.

**The Intuition:** Conduction is like a relay race where runners pass a baton hand-to-hand — each molecule nudges its neighbor. Convection is a crowd forming a current, carrying warmth with the flow. Radiation is a shout across an empty room — energy travels as waves through the void, no contact needed.

**The Math:** Conduction rate is $\frac{Q}{t} = \frac{kA\Delta T}{d}$, where $k$ is thermal conductivity, $A$ is cross-sectional area, $\Delta T$ is temperature difference, and $d$ is thickness. Radiation follows Stefan-Boltzmann: $P = \epsilon \sigma A T^4$ with $\sigma = 5.67 \times 10^{-8}$ W/(m²·K⁴). Carnot efficiency gives the theoretical maximum: $e = 1 - \frac{T_{cold}}{T_{hot}}$.

| Formula | Variables |
|---------|-----------|
| $\frac{Q}{t} = \frac{kA\Delta T}{d}$ | Conduction rate |
| $P = \epsilon \sigma A T^4$ | Radiation (Stefan-Boltzmann) |
| $e = 1 - \frac{T_{cold}}{T_{hot}}$ | Carnot efficiency |

**What does this mean for Physics?** The $T^4$ dependence in radiation means hot objects radiate *dramatically* more than warm ones — double the temperature, sixteen times the power output. This is why stove coils glow red-hot while your coffee steams invisibly. Carnot efficiency tells you the absolute ceiling on any heat engine: no technology, no matter how clever, can beat $1 - \frac{T_C}{T_H}$.

**Setup:** A metal plate ($k = 200$ W/m·K, area 0.1 m², thickness 0.02 m) has $\Delta T = 60$ K. Find the heat flow rate.

**Solution:** $\frac{Q}{t} = \frac{200 \times 0.1 \times 60}{0.02} = 60{,}000$ W $= 60$ kW.

**Key insight:** That's a massive heat flow from a modest temperature difference — thermal conductivity matters enormously in building insulation, heat sink design, and why [[03-Thermal-Physics/03.5-Second-Law|entropy]] always increases in isolated systems.

---

**Setup:** A black body at 2000 K has surface area 0.01 m². Find the power radiated. What power would it radiate at 4000 K? ($\sigma = 5.67 \times 10^{-8}$ W/(m²·K⁴))

**Solution:**
- At 2000 K: $P = \sigma A T^4 = 5.67 \times 10^{-8} \times 0.01 \times (2000)^4 = 5.67 \times 10^{-8} \times 0.01 \times 1.6 \times 10^{13} = 9{,}072$ W $\approx 9.1$ kW.
- At 4000 K: $P = 5.67 \times 10^{-8} \times 0.01 \times (4000)^4 = 5.67 \times 10^{-8} \times 0.01 \times 2.56 \times 10^{14} = 145{,}152$ W $\approx 145$ kW.

**Key insight:** Doubling the temperature increases radiated power by a factor of $2^4 = 16$. This extreme sensitivity is why hot stars are so luminous and why thermal radiation is negligible at everyday temperatures.

---

**Setup:** A wall has thermal conductivity 0.8 W/(m·K), thickness 0.2 m, area 10 m². Inside temperature is 20°C, outside is −5°C. Find the heat loss rate and daily energy loss.

**Solution:**
- Heat loss rate: $\frac{Q}{t} = \frac{kA\Delta T}{d} = \frac{0.8 \times 10 \times 25}{0.2} = 1{,}000$ W = 1 kW.
- Daily energy loss: $E = \frac{Q}{t} \times t = 1{,}000 \times 86{,}400 = 86{,}400{,}000$ J = 86.4 MJ $\approx 24$ kWh.

**Key insight:** A single wall losing 1 kW means the heating system must constantly replace that energy. Better insulation (lower $k$ or greater $d$) reduces this directly — this is the physics behind building energy codes and double-glazing windows.

## Common Mistakes

- ❌ Forgetting that Stefan-Boltzmann uses absolute temperature (Kelvin)
- ❌ Confusing rate of heat flow ($Q/t$) with total heat ($Q$)
- ❌ Forgetting that $\epsilon = 1$ for black body ($\epsilon < 1$ for real objects)
- ❌ Confusing conduction with convection
