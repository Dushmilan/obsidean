# 4.4 Wave Optics

When light passes through narrow slits or around edges, it bends, spreads, and interferes with itself. Geometric optics can't explain this — you need the wave model. Interference, diffraction, and polarization are light revealing its true nature.

**The Intuition:** Throw two pebbles into a still pond at the same time. The ripples overlap — some points get bigger (constructive interference), some cancel out (destructive interference). Light does the same thing, just much faster and smaller.

**The Math:** For a double slit with separation $d$, bright fringes appear where:

$d\sin\theta = n\lambda \quad (n = 0, 1, 2, \ldots)$

Dark fringes fall between:

$d\sin\theta = \left(n + \frac{1}{2}\right)\lambda$

A single slit of width $a$ produces dark minima at:

$a\sin\theta = n\lambda$

After passing through a polarizer, intensity follows Malus's law:

$I = I_0\cos^2\phi$

**What does this mean for Physics?** Wave optics is why we can't focus on infinitely small objects — diffraction sets a fundamental resolution limit. It also underpins [[04-Waves-Optics/04.5-Optical-Instruments|optical instrument design]] and connects directly to [[04-Waves-Optics/04.6-Electromagnetic-Waves|EM wave theory]].

---

**Worked Example 1:**

**Setup:** Double slit with $d = 0.2$ mm, light of $\lambda = 500$ nm. Find the angle to the first maximum.

**Solution:**

$\sin\theta = \frac{n\lambda}{d} = \frac{1 \times 500 \times 10^{-9}}{0.2 \times 10^{-3}} = 0.0025$

$\theta = 0.14°$

**Key insight:** The fringe spacing is tiny — you need a screen metres away to see separation with the naked eye. This is why Young's original experiment was so revolutionary: it proved light is a wave.

---

**Worked Example 2:**

**Setup:** A single slit of width 0.1 mm is illuminated with light of wavelength 600 nm. Find the angle to the first dark minimum and the angular width of the central maximum.

**Solution:**

For the first dark minimum ($n = 1$):

$a\sin\theta = n\lambda$

$\sin\theta = \frac{1 \times 600 \times 10^{-9}}{0.1 \times 10^{-3}} = 0.006$

$\theta = 0.34°$

The central maximum spans from the first minimum on one side to the first minimum on the other side, so:

Angular width $= 2\theta = 2 \times 0.34° = 0.69°$

**Key insight:** The central maximum in single-slit diffraction is twice as wide as the other maxima. It contains most of the light energy, which is why single-slit patterns have a bright centre with fainter side fringes.

---

**Worked Example 3:**

**Setup:** A diffraction grating has 500 lines per mm. Find the angle to the second-order maximum for light of wavelength 589 nm. How many orders can be observed?

**Solution:**

Grating spacing: $d = \frac{1}{500} \text{ mm} = 2 \times 10^{-3} \text{ mm} = 2 \times 10^{-6}$ m

For the second-order maximum ($n = 2$):

$d\sin\theta = n\lambda$

$\sin\theta = \frac{2 \times 589 \times 10^{-9}}{2 \times 10^{-6}} = 0.589$

$\theta = 36.1°$

Maximum observable order: $\sin\theta \leq 1$, so $n_{max} = \left\lfloor\frac{d}{\lambda}\right\rfloor = \left\lfloor\frac{2 \times 10^{-6}}{589 \times 10^{-9}}\right\rfloor = \lfloor 3.40 \rfloor = 3$

Three orders ($n = 1, 2, 3$) can be observed.

**Key insight:** Diffraction grating peaks are much sharper than double-slit fringes because hundreds of slits contribute to constructive interference. This sharpness is what makes gratings useful for separating wavelengths in spectroscopy.

---

**Diffraction Grating:** Replace two slits with hundreds and the maxima become sharp, brilliant lines. The grating equation $d\sin\theta = n\lambda$ is identical to double-slit, but the constructive interference from many slits concentrates light into narrow peaks — the basis of spectroscopy.

---

## Common Mistakes

- ❌ Confusing single-slit dark minima ($a\sin\theta = n\lambda$) with double-slit bright maxima ($d\sin\theta = n\lambda$) — the conditions describe opposite things
- ❌ Forgetting that fringe spacing increases with wavelength — red light ($\lambda \approx 700$ nm) gives wider fringes than blue light ($\lambda \approx 450$ nm)
- ❌ Using wrong order number $n$ — remember $n = 0$ is the central maximum, $n = 1$ is the first-order, etc.
- ❌ Forgetting that diffraction grating peaks are sharper than double-slit fringes — more slits means sharper peaks
