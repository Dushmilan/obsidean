# 4.1 Wave Motion

Waves are how energy gets from A to B without anything actually making the trip. Drop a stone in a pond — the ripples travel outward, but the water mostly just bobs up and down. That's the core idea: waves move energy, not matter.

**The Intuition:** Think of a stadium wave. People stand and sit in place, but the pattern races around the stadium. Each person only moves vertically, yet the wave propagates horizontally. That's transverse wave motion in a nutshell.

**The Math:** The fundamental wave speed relation ties everything together:

$v = f\lambda$

where $f$ is frequency (Hz) and $\lambda$ is wavelength (m). For a wave on a stretched string, the speed depends on the tension and linear density:

$v = \sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}}$

The full wave equation describes displacement $y$ at position $x$ and time $t$:

$y = A\sin(kx - \omega t)$

where $k = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}$ is the wave number and $\omega = 2\pi f$ is the angular frequency.

**What does this mean for Physics?** Everything from ocean waves to light to quantum probability amplitudes uses this framework. The wave equation is the backbone of [[04-Waves-Optics/04.2-Sound|sound]], [[04-Waves-Optics/04.4-Wave-Optics|interference]], and ultimately [[04-Waves-Optics/04.7-Quantum-Nature|quantum mechanics]].

---

**Worked Example 1:**

**Setup:** A wave has frequency 500 Hz and wavelength 0.68 m. Find the speed.

**Solution:**

$v = f\lambda = 500 \times 0.68 = 340 \text{ m/s}$

**Key insight:** This is roughly the speed of sound in air — not a coincidence, since sound waves in air are longitudinal pressure waves governed by the same $v = f\lambda$ relation.

---

**Worked Example 2:**

**Setup:** A wave on a string has equation $y = 0.05\sin(2\pi(0.5x - 10t))$ where $x$ is in metres and $t$ in seconds. Find the amplitude, wavelength, frequency, and wave speed.

**Solution:**

Comparing with $y = A\sin(kx - \omega t)$, we identify $k = 2\pi(0.5) = \pi$ rad/m and $\omega = 2\pi(10) = 20\pi$ rad/s.

Amplitude: $A = 0.05$ m

Wavelength: $\lambda = \frac{2\pi}{k} = \frac{2\pi}{\pi} = 2$ m

Frequency: $f = \frac{\omega}{2\pi} = \frac{20\pi}{2\pi} = 10$ Hz

Wave speed: $v = f\lambda = 10 \times 2 = 20$ m/s

**Key insight:** Always match the given equation to the standard form to extract $k$ and $\omega$. The coefficient inside the sine that multiplies $x$ is $k$, and the one multiplying $t$ is $\omega$.

---

**Worked Example 3:**

**Setup:** A stretched string has linear density $0.005$ kg/m and is under tension $200$ N. Find the wave speed. If the string is $2$ m long, find the wavelength of the fundamental standing wave.

**Solution:**

$v = \sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}} = \sqrt{\frac{200}{0.005}} = \sqrt{40000} = 200 \text{ m/s}$

For the fundamental standing wave on a string fixed at both ends, the wavelength is:

$\lambda = 2L = 2 \times 2 = 4 \text{ m}$

The corresponding frequency is: $f = \frac{v}{\lambda} = \frac{200}{4} = 50$ Hz

**Key insight:** The fundamental has one antinode and nodes at both fixed ends — only half a wavelength fits on the string. Higher harmonics fit integer multiples of half-wavelengths.

---

**Standing Waves:** When two identical waves travel in opposite directions, they form standing waves. Nodes (zero displacement) and antinodes (maximum displacement) appear at fixed positions. This is how musical instruments produce specific frequencies — the boundary conditions select which wavelengths "fit."

---

## Common Mistakes

- ❌ Confusing wave number $k$ with frequency $f$ — $k = 2\pi/\lambda$ is spatial frequency, $f$ is temporal frequency
- ❌ Forgetting that $v = f\lambda$ applies to all waves, not just sound — light, water waves, and seismic waves all use it
- ❌ Mixing up transverse and longitudinal waves — transverse waves oscillate perpendicular to propagation, longitudinal waves oscillate parallel
- ❌ Using angular frequency $\omega$ instead of frequency $f$ in $v = f\lambda$ — remember $\omega = 2\pi f$
