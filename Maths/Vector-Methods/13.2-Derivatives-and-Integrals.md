# Derivatives and Integrals of Vector Functions

Vector functions are just three scalar functions glued together, so differentiation and integration extend naturally: differentiate each component, integrate each component. If $\mathbf{r}(t)$ is the position of a moving car, then $\mathbf{r}'(t)$ is its velocity — it points in the direction of motion and its magnitude is the speed.

**The Intuition:** At each point on the curve, lay down an arrow pointing in the direction the curve is moving. That arrow is $\mathbf{r}'(t)$. Shrink it to unit length and you have the unit tangent $\mathbf{T}(t)$. The derivative measures rate of change; for a vector, that rate of change is itself a vector.

**The Math:** For $\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle f(t), g(t), h(t) \rangle$:

- **Derivative:** $\mathbf{r}'(t) = \langle f'(t), g'(t), h'(t) \rangle$
- **Unit tangent:** $\mathbf{T}(t) = \dfrac{\mathbf{r}'(t)}{\|\mathbf{r}'(t)\|}$
- **Product rules:** $\frac{d}{dt}[\mathbf{r} \cdot \mathbf{s}] = \mathbf{r}' \cdot \mathbf{s} + \mathbf{r} \cdot \mathbf{s}'$ and $\frac{d}{dt}[\mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{s}] = \mathbf{r}' \times \mathbf{s} + \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{s}'$ (order matters in the cross product)
- **Integral:** $\int_a^b \mathbf{r}(t)\,dt = \left\langle \int_a^b f,\; \int_a^b g,\; \int_a^b h \right\rangle$
- **Fundamental theorem:** $\int_a^b \mathbf{r}'(t)\,dt = \mathbf{r}(b) - \mathbf{r}(a)$



**Setup:** $\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle t^2,\; \sin t,\; e^{-t} \rangle$. Find $\mathbf{v}(t)$ and $\mathbf{T}(t)$.

**Solution:** $\mathbf{v}(t) = \langle 2t,\; \cos t,\; -e^{-t} \rangle$. Speed: $\|\mathbf{v}\| = \sqrt{4t^2 + \cos^2 t + e^{-2t}}$. Unit tangent: $\mathbf{T}(t) = \dfrac{\mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{v}\|}$.

**Key insight:** Always compute velocity first, then normalize for direction.

**Setup:** $\mathbf{r}'(t) = \langle 2t, 3t^2, \cos t \rangle$ and $\mathbf{r}(0) = \langle 1, 0, 2 \rangle$. Find $\mathbf{r}(1)$.

**Solution:** $\mathbf{r}(1) = \mathbf{r}(0) + \int_0^1 \mathbf{r}'(t)\,dt = \langle 1, 0, 2 \rangle + \langle 1, 1, \sin 1 \rangle = \langle 2, 1, 2 + \sin 1 \rangle$.

**Key insight:** The FTC for vectors is just three scalar FTCs side by side. The initial condition $\mathbf{r}(0)$ is a vector offset.
