Connected particles problems are Newton's second law applied to systems rather than single objects. The key insight is that you never treat the whole system as one object. Instead, you draw a separate free-body diagram for each particle, write $F = ma$ for each, and then connect them through constraints: the string is inextensible (same acceleration magnitude on both sides) and the pulley is smooth (same tension on both sides). The difficulty varies from simple two-mass Atwood machines to multi-pulley block-and-tackle systems, but the strategy is always the same: FBDs, equations, constraints, solve.

**The Intuition:** Think of a tug-of-war. Two teams pull on a rope, and the rope transmits the force. If the rope does not stretch, both teams must move at the same speed — that is the constraint. The tension is the same throughout a smooth rope. Each particle is in its own world with its own FBD. The string is the only link between their worlds. It pulls on both with the same magnitude.

**The Math:** For two particles, one horizontal ($m_1$) and one hanging ($m_2$), connected by string over a smooth pulley:

$m_1$ (horizontal): $T = m_1 a$. $m_2$ (vertical): $m_2 g - T = m_2 a$. Solving: $a = \frac{m_2 g}{m_1 + m_2}$, $T = \frac{m_1 m_2 g}{m_1 + m_2}$.

For two particles on inclined planes (assuming $m_1$ moves up): $a = \frac{m_2 \sin\theta_2 - m_1 \sin\theta_1}{m_1 + m_2} g$.

When particles are connected by a single inextensible string, their acceleration magnitudes are equal. When a string passes around a pulley or is attached to a fixed point, differentiate the geometric relationship twice to find the acceleration constraint. A movable pulley has mechanical advantage 2: $2T = W$ statically, or $2T - W = (W/g)a$ for acceleration.

**What does this mean for Applied Mechanics?** Massive strings and rough pulleys add complications where tension varies along the string or differs across the pulley, but these are less common at A-level. Always draw a separate FBD for each particle, choose the direction of motion (assume one, solve, check sign of $a$), and write $F = ma$ for each particle along its direction of motion.

**Setup:** $m_1 = 2$ kg, $m_2 = 5$ kg connected by light string over smooth pulley. Find acceleration and tension.

**Solution:** Assume $m_2$ moves down (heavier): $m_2 g - T = m_2 a$ and $T - m_1 g = m_1 a$. Adding: $(m_2 - m_1)g = (m_1 + m_2)a$. $a = (5 - 2)/(5 + 2)(9.8) = 3(9.8)/7 = 4.2$ m/s². $T = m_1(g + a) = 2(9.8 + 4.2) = 28$ N.

**Key insight:** Always assume the direction of motion based on which mass is heavier, then check that $a$ comes out positive. If it is negative, you assumed wrong.

**Setup:** $m_1 = 3$ kg on smooth incline at 30°, $m_2 = 5$ kg on smooth incline at 60°. Connected by string over pulley at the top. Find acceleration.

**Solution:** Assume $m_2$ moves down (heavier and steeper): $m_2 g \sin 60° - T = m_2 a$ and $T - m_1 g \sin 30° = m_1 a$. Adding: $(m_2 \sin 60° - m_1 \sin 30°)g = (m_1 + m_2)a$. $a = (5 \sin 60° - 3 \sin 30°)/(5 + 3)(9.8) = (4.33 - 1.5)/8 \times 9.8 = 3.46$ m/s².

**Key insight:** On an incline, the component along the plane is $mg\sin\theta$, not $mg$. The direction of motion depends on which "effective weight" is larger.
