Work, energy, and power provide an alternative to direct force methods for solving dynamics. Instead of tracking acceleration at every point, you compare the state at the start and end. The work-energy theorem says the net work done equals the change in kinetic energy. If only conservative forces act (gravity, springs), mechanical energy is conserved: $K + U = \text{constant}$. Power measures how fast work is done. This approach is especially powerful for problems involving speed changes over distances (where you do not care about time), vertical motion (gravity as a conservative force), and problems with friction (where you account for energy lost).

**The Intuition:** Imagine pushing a box across a floor. The harder you push (more force) and the further you push it (more distance), the more energy you transfer. That is work. If the box speeds up, that energy went into its kinetic energy. If friction slows it down, energy went into heat. Energy is like a bank account: work done by external forces is a deposit or withdrawal. Conservative forces (gravity, springs) move money between savings (potential) and checking (kinetic). Non-conservative forces (friction) are fees that leave the system.

**The Math:** Work done by a constant force: $W = \vec{F} \cdot \vec{s} = Fs\cos\theta$. For variable force: $W = \int_{\vec{r}_1}^{\vec{r}_2} \vec{F} \cdot d\vec{r}$.

Work-Energy Theorem: $W_{\text{net}} = \Delta K = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 - \frac{1}{2}mu^2$.

Kinetic energy: $K = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 = \frac{p^2}{2m}$. Gravitational PE: $U_g = mgh$. Elastic PE: $U_s = \frac{1}{2}kx^2$.

Conservation of mechanical energy: $E = K + U = \text{constant}$ (conservative forces only). With non-conservative forces: $K_f + U_f = K_i + U_i + W_{\text{nc}}$.

Power: $P_{\text{avg}} = \frac{W}{\Delta t}$, $P = \frac{dW}{dt} = \vec{F} \cdot \vec{v}$. For constant force and velocity: $P = Fv\cos\theta$.

Normal reactions do zero work (perpendicular to displacement). Tensions in inextensible strings do zero work. Friction does negative work $W = -\mu N s$ (always opposes sliding). The key skill is identifying which forces do work and which do not.

**What does this mean for Applied Mechanics?** Energy methods do not replace Newton's laws — they complement them. Use energy methods when you know speeds and distances but not time. Use $F = ma$ when you need time or acceleration at intermediate points. Always define your reference level for gravitational PE before writing equations. Account for friction separately: $K_f + U_f = K_i + U_i - \mu N s$.

**Setup:** A 2 kg ball is dropped from 10 m. Find speed just before hitting the ground (ignore air resistance).

**Solution:** Conservation of energy: $K_i + U_i = K_f + U_f$. At top: $K_i = 0$, $U_i = mgh = 2(9.8)(10) = 196$ J. At bottom: $K_f = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$, $U_f = 0$. $0 + 196 = \frac{1}{2}(2)v^2 + 0$, so $v^2 = 196$. Result: $v = 14$ m/s.

**Key insight:** You do not need to find acceleration or time. Energy methods go directly from start state to end state.

**Setup:** A 1000 kg car travels at 20 m/s on a level road. Brakes apply a friction force of 5000 N. How far does the car skid before stopping?

**Solution:** Work-energy theorem: $W_{\text{net}} = \Delta K$. $W_{\text{friction}} = -5000s$. $\Delta K = 0 - \frac{1}{2}(1000)(20^2) = -200000$ J. $-5000s = -200000$, so $s = 40$ m.

**Key insight:** Work done by friction is negative, and it equals the loss of kinetic energy. The sign convention handles itself if you are careful.
