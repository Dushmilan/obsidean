Impulse and momentum are the time-integral version of Newton's second law. Where work-energy integrates $F = ma$ over distance, impulse-momentum integrates over time. This makes it natural for problems involving impacts, collisions, and forces that act for short durations. The central principle is conservation of momentum: if the net external force on a system is zero, total momentum is constant. In a collision, internal forces are much larger than external forces (weight, friction), so momentum is approximately conserved during the brief impact.

**The Intuition:** A cricket ball hitting a bat. The ball's momentum changes suddenly because the bat exerts a huge force for a tiny time. The impulse (force times time) equals the change in momentum. The bat also recoils slightly because momentum is conserved in the ball-bat system. Momentum is "motion content." In a collision, motion content is shared between objects but never created or destroyed. The total before equals the total after.

**The Math:** Linear momentum: $\vec{p} = m\vec{v}$. Newton's Second Law (momentum form): $\sum \vec{F} = \frac{d\vec{p}}{dt}$.

Impulse: $\vec{I} = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} \vec{F}(t)\,dt = \Delta \vec{p} = \vec{p}_f - \vec{p}_i$. For constant force: $\vec{I} = \vec{F}\Delta t = m(\vec{v}_f - \vec{v}_i)$. Area under $F$-$t$ graph = impulse.

Conservation of momentum: $\sum \vec{F}_{\text{ext}} = 0 \implies \vec{P}_{\text{total}} = \text{constant}$. For two particles: $m_1\vec{u}_1 + m_2\vec{u}_2 = m_1\vec{v}_1 + m_2\vec{v}_2$.

Coefficient of restitution: $e = \frac{v_{2n} - v_{1n}}{u_{1n} - u_{2n}}$ (ratio of separation speed to approach speed along line of centres).

Direct collision equations: Momentum: $m_1u_1 + m_2u_2 = m_1v_1 + m_2v_2$. Restitution: $v_2 - v_1 = e(u_1 - u_2)$.

KE loss: $\Delta K = -\frac{1}{2}\frac{m_1m_2}{m_1+m_2}(1-e^2)(u_1 - u_2)^2$.

During impact, impulsive forces are enormous and act for microseconds. Non-impulsive forces (weight, friction) are finite and can be ignored during the impact itself. Conservation of momentum must hold in each direction separately. In oblique collisions, resolve into normal (along line of centres) and tangential (perpendicular) components. Restitution only applies along the normal; tangential components are unchanged for smooth surfaces.

**What does this mean for Applied Mechanics?** For collisions, write two equations: momentum conservation and the restitution equation. Solve simultaneously. During impact, ignore weight and friction — they are non-impulsive. Always check your answer by verifying momentum is conserved. Check KE if $e = 1$ (should be conserved) or $e = 0$ (should be lost).

**Setup:** $m_1 = 3$ kg moving at $u_1 = 4$ m/s hits $m_2 = 1$ kg at rest ($u_2 = 0$). $e = 1$ (elastic). Find final velocities.

**Solution:** Momentum: $3(4) + 1(0) = 3v_1 + v_2$, so $12 = 3v_1 + v_2$. Restitution: $v_2 - v_1 = 1(4 - 0) = 4$, so $v_2 = v_1 + 4$. Substituting: $12 = 3v_1 + (v_1 + 4) = 4v_1 + 4$, so $v_1 = 2$ m/s. $v_2 = 2 + 4 = 6$ m/s.

**Key insight:** Check: initial KE = $\frac{1}{2}(3)(16) = 24$ J. Final KE = $\frac{1}{2}(3)(4) + \frac{1}{2}(1)(36) = 6 + 18 = 24$ J. KE is conserved as expected for $e = 1$.

**Setup:** A particle hits a smooth wall at 30° to the normal with speed 10 m/s. $e = 0.8$. Find speed and direction after impact.

**Solution:** Normal component before: $u_n = 10\cos 30° = 8.66$ m/s. Tangential component before: $u_t = 10\sin 30° = 5$ m/s. After: $v_n = -eu_n = -0.8(8.66) = -6.93$ m/s (opposite direction). $v_t = u_t = 5$ m/s (unchanged, smooth wall). Rebound speed: $v = \sqrt{6.93^2 + 5^2} = \sqrt{48.02 + 25} = 8.54$ m/s. Angle: $\tan\theta' = 5/6.93 = 0.721$, so $\theta' = 35.8°$.

**Key insight:** The wall is smooth, so tangential momentum is conserved (no friction impulse). Only the normal component changes.
