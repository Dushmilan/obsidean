Collisions are the primary application of impulse-momentum theory. Every collision problem uses the same two equations: momentum is conserved (net external force is zero during impact), and the coefficient of restitution links the relative speeds before and after along the line of centres. The classification is straightforward: elastic ($e = 1$, KE conserved), inelastic ($0 < e < 1$, some KE lost), and perfectly inelastic ($e = 0$, maximum KE lost, objects stick). Direct collisions are one-dimensional and give closed-form solutions. Oblique collisions require resolving into normal and tangential components.

**The Intuition:** Dropping a steel ball (bouncy, $e$ close to 1) vs dropping a lump of putty (not bouncy, $e$ close to 0). The steel ball bounces back nearly as high as it was dropped. The putty splats and does not bounce at all. The coefficient $e$ measures this bounciness. In a collision, momentum is always shared between the objects. How much kinetic energy survives the sharing depends on $e$. Elastic means perfect sharing with no loss. Inelastic means some energy goes into heat, sound, or permanent deformation.

**The Math:** Direct collision equations: Momentum: $m_1u_1 + m_2u_2 = m_1v_1 + m_2v_2$. Restitution: $v_2 - v_1 = e(u_1 - u_2)$.

Solutions: $v_1 = \frac{m_1 - em_2}{m_1 + m_2}u_1 + \frac{m_2(1+e)}{m_1 + m_2}u_2$, $v_2 = \frac{m_1(1+e)}{m_1 + m_2}u_1 + \frac{m_2 - em_1}{m_1 + m_2}u_2$.

KE loss: $\Delta K = -\frac{1}{2}\frac{m_1m_2}{m_1+m_2}(1-e^2)(u_1 - u_2)^2$.

Wall collision (smooth plane at angle $\theta$): Normal: $u_n = u\cos\theta$, $v_n = -eu\cos\theta$. Tangential: $u_t = u\sin\theta$, $v_t = u\sin\theta$. Rebound angle: $\tan\theta' = \tan\theta / e$. Speed after: $v = u\sqrt{\sin^2\theta + e^2\cos^2\theta}$. Impulse on wall: $I = mu\cos\theta(1+e)$.

Ballistic pendulum: $v = \frac{M+m}{m}\sqrt{2gh}$.

Restitution only applies along the line of centres. In oblique collisions, tangential components are unchanged for smooth surfaces. After solving the first collision, check whether the resulting velocities lead to another collision — the approaching particle must actually be faster than the one in front. A wall collision is the limit where $m_2 \to \infty$ and $u_2 = 0$.

**What does this mean for Applied Mechanics?** For direct collisions, write momentum and restitution equations, then solve simultaneously. For oblique collisions, resolve into normal and tangential components first. For wall collisions, treat the wall as having infinite mass. For the ballistic pendulum, separate the problem into two stages: inelastic collision (momentum conserved, KE not), then swing (energy conserved, momentum not).

**Setup:** $m_1 = 2$ kg at $u_1 = 5$ m/s hits $m_2 = 3$ kg at $u_2 = -1$ m/s (moving toward $m_1$). $e = 1$. Find final velocities.

**Solution:** Momentum: $2(5) + 3(-1) = 2v_1 + 3v_2$, so $7 = 2v_1 + 3v_2$. Restitution: $v_2 - v_1 = 1(5 - (-1)) = 6$, so $v_2 = v_1 + 6$. Substituting: $7 = 2v_1 + 3(v_1 + 6) = 5v_1 + 18$, so $v_1 = -11/5 = -2.2$ m/s. $v_2 = -2.2 + 6 = 3.8$ m/s.

**Key insight:** The negative sign means $m_1$ reverses direction. Check momentum: $2(-2.2) + 3(3.8) = -4.4 + 11.4 = 7$. Correct.

**Setup:** A 10 g bullet embeds in a 1.99 kg wooden block suspended as a pendulum. The block swings to a height of 8 cm. Find the bullet's initial speed.

**Solution:** Swing stage (energy conservation): $\frac{1}{2}(M+m)V^2 = (M+m)gh$. $V = \sqrt{2gh} = \sqrt{2(9.8)(0.08)} = 1.252$ m/s. Collision stage (momentum conservation): $mv = (M+m)V$. $v = \frac{M+m}{m}V = \frac{1.99 + 0.01}{0.01}(1.252) = 200(1.252) = 250.4$ m/s.

**Key insight:** The two stages use different conservation laws: momentum for the collision (energy is not conserved in inelastic impact), then energy for the swing (no friction, so mechanical energy is conserved).
