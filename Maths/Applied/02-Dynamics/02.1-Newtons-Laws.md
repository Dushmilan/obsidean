Before we can analyse how objects move under forces, we need the rules that connect them. Newton's three laws are the entry point to all of dynamics — they tell you what happens when forces act on a body, and they hold in every inertial frame of reference. In practice, you will almost never use the first or third law in isolation. Instead, you draw a free-body diagram, identify all forces on an object, then apply $F = ma$ component-wise. The skill is in choosing the right coordinate axes and correctly resolving forces along those axes.

**The Intuition:** Imagine pushing a shopping trolley. A heavy trolley (large mass) accelerates slowly for the same push. Push harder (more force) and it speeds up faster. If you push a light trolley the same amount, it shoots forward — that is $F = ma$ in action. A free-body diagram isolates one object and shows every force acting on it as an arrow. The net arrow (vector sum) points in the direction of acceleration. The first law is a special case of the second ($F = 0$ gives $a = 0$). The third law is about symmetry — every interaction involves two objects, so forces must be equal and opposite between them.

**The Math:** The three laws are:

**First Law (Inertia):** $\sum \vec{F} = 0 \implies \vec{a} = 0 \implies \vec{v} = \text{constant}$. Defines inertial frames. Mass $m$ measures inertia (resistance to acceleration).

**Second Law (Acceleration):** $\sum \vec{F} = \frac{d\vec{p}}{dt} = \frac{d(m\vec{v})}{dt}$. For constant mass: $\sum \vec{F} = m\vec{a}$. Component form: $\sum F_x = ma_x$, $\sum F_y = ma_y$.

**Third Law (Action-Reaction):** $\vec{F}_{AB} = -\vec{F}_{BA}$. Forces act on different bodies, are of the same type, and are simultaneous.

A subtlety: third-law pairs act on different bodies, so they never cancel each other out. Normal reaction is not always $mg$ — on an incline, $R = mg\cos\theta$. Never assume $R = mg$ without checking all vertical forces. Static friction opposes the tendency to move, not necessarily the direction of an applied force; kinetic friction opposes the direction of sliding.

**What does this mean for Applied Mechanics?** These laws have limits — they break down at very high speeds (relativity) and very small scales (quantum mechanics) — but for A-level dynamics they are the complete toolkit. The key skills are: always draw a free-body diagram before writing any equations; choose axes that simplify the problem; resolve every force into components; write $F = ma$ for each axis separately.

**Setup:** A 5 kg block sits on a rough horizontal surface ($\mu = 0.3$). A force $F = 30$ N is applied at angle $\theta = 30°$ above the horizontal. Find acceleration.

**Solution:** Resolve $F$ into components: $F_x = 30\cos 30° = 25.98$ N, $F_y = 30\sin 30° = 15$ N. Vertical equilibrium: $R + 15 = 5(9.8)$, so $R = 34$ N. Friction: $f = \mu R = 0.3(34) = 10.2$ N. Horizontal: $F_x - f = ma$, so $25.98 - 10.2 = 5a$. Result: $a = 3.16$ m/s².

**Key insight:** Always resolve the applied force first, then use vertical equilibrium to find $R$ before calculating friction.

**Setup:** Two masses $m_1 = 3$ kg and $m_2 = 5$ kg are connected by a light inextensible string over a smooth pulley. Find acceleration and tension.

**Solution:** Assume $m_2$ moves down (heavier): $m_2 g - T = m_2 a$ and $T - m_1 g = m_1 a$. Adding: $(m_2 - m_1)g = (m_1 + m_2)a$, so $a = (5 - 3)/(5 + 3)(9.8) = 2.45$ m/s². Substituting: $T = m_1(g + a) = 3(9.8 + 2.45) = 36.75$ N.

**Key insight:** The string means both masses have the same acceleration magnitude. Tension is the same on both sides for a smooth pulley.
