When mass is constant, Newton's second law gives $F = ma$. When mass changes, you must go back to the fundamental form: net external force equals the rate of change of momentum. This gives the variable mass equation: $M\frac{dv}{dt} = v_{\text{rel}}\frac{dM}{dt} + \sum F_{\text{ext}}$. The extra term $v_{\text{rel}}\frac{dM}{dt}$ is sometimes called the thrust force. The classic application is the rocket: mass decreases as fuel is ejected, and the thrust force propels the rocket forward.

**The Intuition:** A garden hose on a skateboard. If you spray water backward (mass ejection), the hose moves forward. The more water you spray and the faster you spray it, the more forward force you get. That is the rocket principle. A rocket is constantly throwing mass backward. Each bit of ejected fuel carries momentum backward, so the rocket gains momentum forward. The total momentum of rocket plus fuel is conserved if no external forces act.

**The Math:** General variable mass equation: $M\frac{dv}{dt} = v_{\text{rel}}\frac{dM}{dt} + \sum F_{\text{ext}}$. Sign convention: $dM/dt > 0$ for mass gain, $< 0$ for mass loss. $v_{\text{rel}}$ is the velocity of added/removed mass relative to the system.

Rocket equation (free space): $v = u\ln\frac{M_0}{M} + v_0$. Tsiolkovsky: $\Delta v = u\ln\frac{M_0}{M_f}$, where $u$ = exhaust speed, $M_0$ = initial mass, $M_f$ = final mass.

Rocket with gravity (vertical): $v = u\ln\frac{M_0}{M} - gt$.

Chain falling onto scale: $F = 3\lambda gx$ (three times the weight of chain on table). Chain sliding off table: $v^2 = \frac{g}{L}(x^2 - x_0^2)$. Chain lifted at constant speed $v$: $F = \lambda gx + \lambda v^2$. Raindrop accretion: $\frac{d}{dt}(mv) = mg$.

The thrust force is not an external force — it arises from the momentum carried by the added or removed mass. For a rocket, the thrust is internal to the rocket-fuel system but acts as an external force on the rocket alone. The term $v_{\text{rel}}dM/dt$ is not an external force. For a rocket, the thrust force is $u$ times the fuel burn rate ($\alpha = -dM/dt$). This acts in the direction opposite to the exhaust.

**What does this mean for Applied Mechanics?** Always identify whether mass is being gained or lost, and define $v_{\text{rel}}$ correctly. For rockets, the thrust force is $u\alpha$ where $\alpha = -dM/dt$. For chains, separate the problem into the weight of the resting part and the rate of momentum change of the moving part. Check your signs: $dM/dt$ is negative for rockets (mass loss), positive for raindrops (mass gain).

**Setup:** A rocket starts from rest with initial mass $M_0 = 500$ kg (including fuel). It ejects fuel at rate 2 kg/s with exhaust speed $u = 2000$ m/s relative to the rocket. Final mass $M_f = 100$ kg. Find the final speed.

**Solution:** Tsiolkovsky equation: $\Delta v = u\ln(M_0/M_f)$. $\Delta v = 2000\ln(500/100) = 2000\ln 5 = 2000(1.609) = 3219$ m/s.

**Key insight:** The rocket equation is independent of the rate of fuel ejection. What matters is the total mass ratio and the exhaust speed. Faster exhaust or more fuel gives more $\Delta v$.

**Setup:** A 1000 kg rocket (800 kg fuel) launches vertically. Exhaust speed $u = 2500$ m/s, fuel burn rate $\alpha = 10$ kg/s. Find the speed after 20 seconds.

**Solution:** At $t = 20$ s, mass burned = $10(20) = 200$ kg, so $M = 1000 - 200 = 800$ kg. $v = u\ln(M_0/M) - gt = 2500\ln(1000/800) - 9.8(20)$. $v = 2500\ln(1.25) - 196 = 2500(0.2231) - 196 = 557.8 - 196 = 361.8$ m/s.

**Key insight:** Gravity reduces the effective thrust. The rocket must overcome its own weight before it can accelerate upward. The term $-gt$ is the speed lost to gravity during the burn.
