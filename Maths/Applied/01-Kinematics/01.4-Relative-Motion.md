# Relative Motion (1D and 2D)

The velocity or acceleration of one object as observed from another moving object. Many real-world problems — overtaking, collision, river crossing, aircraft navigation — are most naturally solved by considering motion relative to a moving frame. When you are on a train, stationary objects outside appear to move backward. The train's velocity relative to the ground is one thing; the ground's velocity relative to the train is the opposite. This simple idea — subtracting velocities — extends naturally to 2D vectors.

**The Intuition:** Two cars on a motorway. Car A travels at 70 mph, Car B at 60 mph. From Car B's perspective, Car A is moving forward at 10 mph. From Car A's perspective, Car B is moving backward at 10 mph. The relative velocity is just the difference. If both travel in the same direction, it is simple subtraction. If they travel at angles, you need vector subtraction.

Stand on one moving object and observe the other. Your velocity subtracts from everything. If you move at $\vec{v}_B$, then every other object's velocity appears to be $\vec{v} - \vec{v}_B$ relative to you. Newton's laws hold in all inertial (non-accelerating) frames. Subtracting a constant velocity from all objects does not change accelerations or the validity of physical laws. This is Galilean relativity.

**The Math:** In one dimension, relative velocity is just subtraction of signed scalars: $v_{A/B} = v_A - v_B$. In two dimensions, it becomes vector subtraction: $\vec{v}_{A/B} = \vec{v}_A - \vec{v}_B$, with magnitude found using the cosine rule: $|\vec{v}_{A/B}| = \sqrt{v_A^2 + v_B^2 - 2v_A v_B \cos\theta}$.

Relative acceleration follows the same pattern: $\vec{a}_{A/B} = \vec{a}_A - \vec{a}_B$. If both objects have constant acceleration, all SUVAT equations apply to the relative quantities: $s_{A/B} = u_{A/B}t + \frac{1}{2}a_{A/B}t^2$.

For closest approach, minimise $d^2(t) = |\vec{r}_{A/B}(0) + \vec{v}_{A/B}t|^2$. Differentiating and setting to zero gives $t_{\min} = -\frac{\vec{r}_{A/B}(0) \cdot \vec{v}_{A/B}}{|\vec{v}_{A/B}|^2}$. Collision requires the relative position to become zero at some time $t > 0$ — which is different from closest approach, where the distance is minimised but may be non-zero.

For objects connected by an inextensible string over a pulley, the string length is constant. Differentiating once gives a velocity constraint; differentiating twice gives an acceleration constraint. These constraints are the starting point for connected-particle dynamics.

**What does this mean for mechanics?** The power comes from choosing the right frame: sometimes the ground frame is natural, sometimes a moving frame simplifies the problem. The closest approach formula is especially useful in exam contexts — it turns a geometric minimisation problem into a direct formula.

**Worked Examples:**

**Example 1: Overtaking — 1D Relative Motion**

**Setup:** Car A starts from rest and accelerates at 2 m/s². Car B passes A at $t=0$ travelling at constant 10 m/s. A is initially stationary. When does A overtake B?

**Solution:** Position of A: $s_A = \frac{1}{2}(2)t^2 = t^2$. Position of B: $s_B = 10t$. Overtake when $s_A = s_B$: $t^2 = 10t \implies t(t - 10) = 0$. So $t = 0$ (start) or $t = 10$ s.

**Key insight:** At $t=10$, $s_A = 100$ m. After this, A is ahead and pulling away because its velocity ($v_A = 2t = 20$ m/s at $t=10$) exceeds B's constant 10 m/s.

**Example 2: Closest Approach — 2D**

**Setup:** Ship A sails east at 15 km/h from point $P$. Ship B sails north at 20 km/h from point $Q$, which is 50 km due east of $P$. Both start at the same time. Find the minimum distance between the ships.

**Solution:** Position of A: $(15t, 0)$. Position of B: $(50, 20t)$. Distance squared: $d^2 = (50 - 15t)^2 + (20t)^2 = 625t^2 - 1500t + 2500$. Minimise: $\frac{d(d^2)}{dt} = 1250t - 1500 = 0 \implies t = 1.2$ h. $d_{\min}^2 = 625(1.44) - 1500(1.2) + 2500 = 1600$. So $d_{\min} = 40$ km.

**Key insight:** The ships' paths are perpendicular (east and north). The closest approach occurs when the line connecting them is perpendicular to the relative velocity.

**Example 3: River Crossing — Shortest Time**

**Setup:** A river is 200 m wide. Water flows at 3 m/s. A swimmer can swim at 5 m/s in still water. What heading gives shortest crossing time? What is the drift?

**Solution:** Shortest time: swim directly perpendicular to the bank (heading straight across). Time: $t = \frac{200}{5} = 40$ s. Drift: $d_{\text{drift}} = v_{\text{river}} \times t = 3 \times 40 = 120$ m downstream.

**Key insight:** Shortest time and shortest path are different problems. For shortest path (zero drift), you must point upstream at angle $\theta$ where $\sin\theta = v_r/v_b = 3/5$, giving $\theta = 36.9°$ and time = $200/(5\cos 36.9°) = 50$ s.
