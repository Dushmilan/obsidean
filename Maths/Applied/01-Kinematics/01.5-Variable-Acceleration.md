# Variable Acceleration

Motion where acceleration is not constant, requiring calculus instead of SUVAT. Real-world motion almost always has variable acceleration — air resistance, spring forces, orbital mechanics — and SUVAT gives wrong answers here. The approach depends on what acceleration is a function of.

**The Intuition:** Imagine a car where the accelerator pedal is connected to a spring. The harder you press, the more the spring resists, so acceleration depends on how fast you are already going. At low speed, you accelerate quickly. At high speed, acceleration drops. Eventually you reach terminal velocity where drag equals engine force.

Integration is accumulating small changes. If acceleration changes every instant, you add up all the tiny velocity changes ($a\,dt$) over time to get total velocity change. Same for displacement: add up all the tiny displacements ($v\,dt$). The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus guarantees that if you know the rate of change, you can recover the function by integrating.

**The Math:** The three cases depend on what $a$ depends on.

**Case 1: $a = a(t)$ — Direct integration.** $v(t) = u + \int_0^t a(\tau)\,d\tau$ and $s(t) = \int_0^t v(\tau)\,d\tau$. This is the most straightforward case. For example, if $a = kt$ (linear), then $v = u + \frac{1}{2}kt^2$ and $s = ut + \frac{1}{6}kt^3$.

**Case 2: $a = a(v)$ — Separation of variables.** Rearrange $\frac{dv}{dt} = a(v)$ to get $dt = \frac{dv}{a(v)}$ and integrate: $t = \int_u^v \frac{dv'}{a(v')}$. For displacement: $s = \int_u^v \frac{v'\,dv'}{a(v')}$. Linear drag ($a = -kv$) gives $v = ue^{-kt}$ and terminal velocity problems.

**Case 3: $a = a(s)$ — Chain rule form.** Use $a = v\frac{dv}{ds}$, rearrange to $v\,dv = a(s)\,ds$ and integrate: $v^2 = u^2 + 2\int_0^s a(s')\,ds'$. This eliminates time entirely. SHM ($a = -\omega^2 s$) falls into this case, giving $s = A\cos(\omega t + \phi)$ with period $T = 2\pi/\omega$.

A subtlety: the chain rule form $a = v\frac{dv}{cs}$ is not a different formula — it comes from $a = \frac{dv}{dt} = \frac{dv}{ds}\cdot\frac{ds}{dt} = v\frac{dv}{ds}$. After every indefinite integral, immediately apply the initial condition to find the constant. Always check dimensions after integration — this catches errors.

**What does this mean for mechanics?** The hardest part is usually setting up the integral correctly, not evaluating it. When in doubt, go back to the definitions: $v = ds/dt$ and $a = dv/dt$. Everything else is just calculus. The [[01.2-Equations-of-Motion|SUVAT equations]] are the special case when $a$ is constant.

**Worked Examples:**

**Example 1: Rocket with Variable Mass (Tsiolkovsky)**

**Setup:** A rocket of initial mass $M_0$ burns fuel at rate $\mu$ (kg/s) with exhaust speed $u$ relative to the rocket. No external forces. Find the velocity as a function of time.

**Solution:** Momentum conservation: $(M_0 - \mu t)\frac{dv}{dt} = \mu u$. Separate variables: $dv = \frac{\mu u}{M_0 - \mu t}\,dt$. Integrate from $v=0$ at $t=0$: $v = u \ln\!\left(\frac{M_0}{M_0 - \mu t}\right)$.

**Key insight:** Velocity depends only on exhaust speed and mass ratio, not on burn rate. This is the Tsiolkovsky rocket equation.

**Example 2: Vertical Motion with Linear Resistance**

**Setup:** A particle is projected upward with $u = 20$ m/s. Linear drag $a = -g - kv$ with $k = 0.5$ s$^{-1}$ and $g = 10$ m/s$^2$. Find time to reach max height and max height.

**Solution:** Upward motion: $a = -(g + kv)$ since $v > 0$. Separation of variables: $t = -\int_{20}^{0} \frac{dv}{g + kv} = \frac{1}{k}\ln\!\left(\frac{g + k \cdot 20}{g}\right) = 2\ln 2 = 1.386$ s. Velocity: $v(t) = (u + mg/k)e^{-kt} - mg/k = 30e^{-0.5t} - 20$. Displacement: $s = \int_0^{1.386} (30e^{-0.5t'} - 20)\,dt' = 2.28$ m.

**Key insight:** Without drag, $H = u^2/(2g) = 20$ m. With drag, $H$ is drastically reduced. Drag dominates at high speeds.

**Example 3: SHM — Spring-Mass System**

**Setup:** A mass on a spring has $a = -4s$ m/s$^2$ (i.e., $\omega = 2$ rad/s). At $t=0$, $s = 0.1$ m, $v = 0$. Find $s(t)$ and the period.

**Solution:** At $t=0$: $s = 0.1$, $v = 0$, so amplitude $A = 0.1$ m. $s(t) = 0.1\cos(2t)$. Period: $T = 2\pi/\omega = 2\pi/2 = \pi$ seconds.

**Key insight:** The period is independent of amplitude. Whether you pull the mass 0.1 m or 1 m, it oscillates with the same period.
