# Equations of Motion (Constant Acceleration — SUVAT)

Most exam kinematics problems involve constant acceleration — especially gravity — and five equations let you solve them without calculus. These are the workhorse of A-level mechanics, derived by integrating $a = dv/dt$ once to get $v = u + at$, again to get $s = ut + \frac{1}{2}at^2$, with the third obtained by eliminating time. Each equation is "missing" one variable, and the skill in using SUVAT is identifying which equation to use based on what you know and what you need.

**The Intuition:** Imagine a ball rolling down a ramp. If it starts from rest and speeds up by 2 m/s every second, then after 1 second it goes 2 m/s, after 2 seconds it goes 4 m/s, and so on — that is $v = u + at$. The distance it covers is not simply $u \times t$ because it is speeding up; the average speed is $\frac{u+v}{2}$, giving $s = \frac{u+v}{2} \cdot t$. From there, substituting $v = u + at$ gives equation 2.

A velocity-time graph for constant acceleration is a straight line. The area under this line (a trapezium) is the displacement. The slope is the acceleration. All five equations can be read directly from this graph. These equations are the inevitable consequence of $a$ being constant — integration of a constant is linear, which is why all the equations are at most quadratic in $t$.

**The Math:** The five SUVAT equations relate displacement ($s$), initial velocity ($u$), final velocity ($v$), acceleration ($a$), and time ($t$):

$v = u + at$ (missing $s$), $s = ut + \frac{1}{2}at^2$ (missing $v$), $v^2 = u^2 + 2as$ (missing $t$), $s = \frac{1}{2}(u+v)t$ (missing $a$), $s = vt - \frac{1}{2}at^2$ (missing $u$).

The derivation is straightforward calculus. Starting from $a = \frac{dv}{dt}$ (constant): $\int_u^v dv = \int_0^t a\,dt \implies v = u + at$. From $v = \frac{ds}{dt} = u + at$: $\int_0^s ds = \int_0^t (u + at)\,dt \implies s = ut + \frac{1}{2}at^2$. Eliminating $t$ using $t = \frac{v-u}{a}$ gives $v^2 = u^2 + 2as$.

Sign convention is everything. Choose a positive direction at the start and stick to it. For vertical motion, the usual choice is upward = positive, which gives $a = -g$. If you accidentally use $a = g$ with upward as positive, every answer will be wrong. Displacement $s$ can be negative — it means the particle is below or behind the starting point. When a projectile is thrown upward, its velocity is zero at the peak: set $v = 0$ in equation 1 to get $t = \frac{u}{g}$.

The fastest approach to equation selection: list your five quantities ($s$, $u$, $v$, $a$, $t$), note which three you know, which one you want, and which one you do not need. Pick the equation that does not involve the unused variable. This avoids unnecessary algebra.

**What does this mean for mechanics?** The equations assume a flat Earth, uniform gravity, and no air resistance. They fail for variable acceleration (use [[01.5-Variable-Acceleration|calculus methods]] instead) and for relativistic speeds. For everyday-scale problems, they work remarkably well.

**Worked Examples:**

**Example 1: Classic Vertical Projection**

**Setup:** A stone is thrown vertically upward with $u = 20$ m/s from ground level. Take $g = 10$ m/s². Find (a) maximum height, (b) time of flight, (c) velocity on hitting the ground.

**Solution:** Maximum height: set $v = 0$ in $v^2 = u^2 + 2as$: $0 = 400 - 20H \implies H = 20$ m. Time to top: $v = u + at \implies 0 = 20 - 10t \implies t = 2$ s. By symmetry, total time of flight $T = 2 \times 2 = 4$ s. On return to ground, $s = 0$: $0 = 20T - 5T^2 = T(20 - 5T) \implies T = 4$ s. Velocity on return: $v = 20 - 10(4) = -20$ m/s (speed 20 m/s, directed downward).

**Key insight:** The speed on return equals the launch speed (symmetry of constant acceleration). The displacement is zero even though the particle travelled 40 m.

**Example 2: Multi-Stage Motion with Bounce**

**Setup:** A ball is dropped from 4.5 m. On each bounce, it reaches 4/9 of its previous height. Find the total distance travelled before coming to rest.

**Solution:** First drop: distance = 4.5 m. First bounce height: $\frac{4}{9} \times 4.5 = 2$ m. Travels 2 m up and 2 m down = 4 m. Second bounce height: $\frac{4}{9} \times 2 = \frac{8}{9}$ m. This is a geometric series with $a = 4.5$, $r = \frac{8}{9}$. Total distance $= 4.5 + 2(2 + \frac{8}{9} + \frac{64}{81} + \cdots) = 4.5 + 2 \times \frac{2}{1 - 8/9} = 4.5 + 36 = 40.5$ m.

**Key insight:** Each bounce is a separate SUVAT problem, but the geometric series makes the total finite.

**Example 3: Two Particles — Meeting Problem**

**Setup:** Particle A is projected upward from ground with $u_A = 30$ m/s. Particle B is dropped from rest at height 50 m at the same instant. Do they collide? If so, when and where?

**Solution:** Write positions: $y_A = 30t - 5t^2$, $y_B = 50 - 5t^2$. Set equal: $30t - 5t^2 = 50 - 5t^2 \implies 30t = 50 \implies t = \frac{5}{3}$ s. Check $t > 0$ and both still moving: $y_A(\frac{5}{3}) = 30(\frac{5}{3}) - 5(\frac{25}{9}) = 50 - \frac{125}{9} = \frac{325}{9} \approx 36.1$ m. $y_B(\frac{5}{3}) = 50 - \frac{125}{9} = \frac{325}{9}$ m. They match.

**Key insight:** The $-5t^2$ terms cancel because both particles have the same acceleration ($-g$). This is effectively a relative motion problem where gravity drops out.
