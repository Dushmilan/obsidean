# Displacement, Velocity, Acceleration (Rectilinear Motion)

Before we can study forces, energy, or momentum, we need to describe how objects move. Displacement, velocity, and acceleration form a chain: position determines displacement, displacement's rate of change is velocity, and velocity's rate of change is acceleration. Each level is obtained by differentiating the one below it; each level is obtained by integrating the one above it. In one dimension, everything reduces to signed scalars — pick a positive direction, and every quantity becomes a single number that is positive or negative depending on which way it points relative to your chosen axis.

**The Intuition:** Think of a car journey. Your odometer reading (distance) only goes up. Your displacement from home can go up or down — if you drive away then come back, displacement is zero even though the odometer shows miles. The speedometer shows speed (always positive), but velocity carries a sign telling you which direction you're heading.

A number line with a dot sliding along it captures this well. The dot's position is $r$, its displacement from the start is $s = r - r_0$, how fast the dot slides is $v$, and whether it's speeding up or slowing down is $a$. The definitions follow directly from what "instantaneous" means in calculus — the limit of a small change over a small time. This is the only consistent way to define rate of change for non-uniform motion.

**The Math:** Displacement ($s$) is the signed change in position from a reference point: $s = r(t) - r(0)$. Unlike distance, displacement can be negative and satisfies $|s| \leq \text{distance}$. Velocity is the rate of change of displacement: $v = \frac{ds}{dt}$. Speed is the magnitude $|v|$. Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity: $a = \frac{dv}{dt} = \frac{d^2s}{dt^2}$.

The chain rule gives a powerful alternative form: $a = v\frac{dv}{ds}$. This connects acceleration directly to velocity and position without involving time, which is invaluable when time is not given or not asked for. For constant acceleration, the familiar equations emerge: $v = u + at$, $s = ut + \frac{1}{2}at^2$, $v^2 = u^2 + 2as$. These only apply when $a$ is constant — the calculus definitions are always valid.

A subtlety worth noting: displacement is a vector quantity (signed in 1D) while distance is a scalar and always non-negative. If a particle moves from $x=0$ to $x=5$ and back to $x=2$, displacement is $+2$ but distance is $8$. Similarly, "deceleration" means $a$ and $v$ have opposite signs, not that $a$ is negative. Check whether they share the same sign: same sign means speeding up, opposite signs means slowing down.

**What does this mean for mechanics?** These three quantities are the foundation of all kinematics. Every problem reduces to relating $s$, $v$, and $a$ under given conditions. The constant-acceleration formulae are the workhorse for most exam problems, but when acceleration varies, you must integrate. Always ask: "Is acceleration constant? Is time given or needed?" That one question determines your entire approach.

**Worked Examples:**

**Example 1: Variable Acceleration — Finding Velocity and Position**

**Setup:** A particle moves along the $x$-axis with $a = 6t - 12$ m/s². At $t=0$, $x=0$, $v=9$ m/s. Find when it comes to rest and its position at those times.

**Solution:** Integrate acceleration to get velocity: $v = 9 + \int_0^t (6t' - 12)\,dt' = 9 + 3t^2 - 12t = 3(t-1)(t-3)$. Set $v = 0$: $t = 1$ s or $t = 3$ s. Integrate velocity to get position: $x = \int_0^t (3t'^2 - 12t' + 9)\,dt' = t^3 - 6t^2 + 9t$. At $t=1$: $x = 4$ m. At $t=3$: $x = 0$ m.

**Key insight:** The particle moves forward, stops, reverses, and returns to the origin. Displacement and distance diverge after the first turning point.

**Example 2: Chain Rule Form — No Time Given**

**Setup:** A particle has acceleration $a = -2v$ m/s² (linear drag). At $s=0$, $v=10$ m/s. Find $v$ as a function of $s$.

**Solution:** Use $a = v\frac{dv}{ds}$: $-2v = v\frac{dv}{ds}$. Cancel $v$ (valid since $v \neq 0$ initially): $\frac{dv}{ds} = -2$. Integrate: $v = 10 - 2s$.

**Key insight:** When the problem does not mention time, the chain rule form $a = v\frac{dv}{ds}$ is often the most direct path. The particle stops when $s = 5$ m.

**Example 3: Average Velocity Trap**

**Setup:** A particle travels at 60 m/s for 10 s, then at 40 m/s for 10 s. What is the average velocity for the whole journey?

**Solution:** Total displacement: $s = 60(10) + 40(10) = 1000$ m. Total time: $t = 20$ s. Average velocity: $v_{\text{avg}} = \frac{1000}{20} = 50$ m/s.

**Key insight:** This equals $\frac{u+v}{2}$ only because each segment has constant velocity. For segments with different durations, $\frac{u+v}{2}$ would be wrong — you must compute total displacement over total time.
