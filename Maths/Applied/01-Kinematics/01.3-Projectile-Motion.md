# Projectile Motion

Two-dimensional motion under gravity alone, where horizontal velocity is constant and vertical motion is uniformly accelerated. It is the simplest 2D kinematics problem and the basis for ballistics, sports science, and satellite orbit approximations. The key insight is that you can decompose a 2D problem into two independent 1D problems.

**The Intuition:** Think of a ball rolling off a table at the same time as an identical ball is dropped vertically from the same height. Both balls hit the ground at the same time. The horizontally-launched ball has horizontal velocity (it travels sideways), but its vertical motion is identical to the dropped ball. This is because horizontal and vertical motions are independent — gravity only acts vertically, so it only changes the vertical component of velocity. The horizontal component has nothing to change it (no air resistance), so it stays constant.

A cannonball follows a parabolic arc. At every point on the arc, the velocity vector is tangent to the path. At the top of the arc, the velocity is purely horizontal (vertical component is zero). The shape is always a parabola because the vertical displacement is quadratic in time.

**The Math:** Decompose the launch velocity into components: $u_x = u \cos \theta$ (horizontal, constant) and $u_y = u \sin \theta$ (vertical, subject to gravity). The horizontal position is $x = (u \cos \theta)t$ and vertical displacement is $y = (u \sin \theta)t - \frac{1}{2}gt^2$.

The standard results follow from SUVAT applied vertically. Time of flight: $T = \frac{2u \sin \theta}{g}$. Maximum height: $H = \frac{u^2 \sin^2 \theta}{2g}$. Range on level ground: $R = \frac{u^2 \sin 2\theta}{g}$. The maximum range occurs at $\theta = 45°$, and complementary angles ($\theta$ and $90° - \theta$) give the same range because $\sin 2\theta = \sin 2(90° - \theta)$.

The trajectory equation eliminates $t$: $y = x \tan \theta - \frac{gx^2}{2u^2 \cos^2 \theta}$. Speed at height $y$ depends only on height, not angle: $v = \sqrt{u^2 - 2gy}$, which follows from energy conservation.

On inclined planes, the formulas change. Range up an incline at angle $\alpha$: $R_{\text{up}} = \frac{u^2 \sin 2(\theta - \alpha)}{g \cos \alpha}$. Range down: $R_{\text{down}} = \frac{u^2 \sin 2(\theta + \alpha)}{g \cos \alpha}$. The optimal angle for maximum range up the plane is $\theta = 45° + \alpha/2$, not 45°.

**What does this mean for mechanics?** Projectile motion is where kinematics becomes visual. The decomposition into horizontal and vertical is one of the most powerful ideas in mechanics — the same principle appears in every subsequent 2D problem: relative motion, inclined planes, circular motion, and beyond.

**Worked Examples:**

**Example 1: Basic Projectile — Range and Height**

**Setup:** A ball is launched at $u = 20$ m/s at angle $\theta = 30°$ to the horizontal. $g = 10$ m/s². Find (a) time of flight, (b) maximum height, (c) range.

**Solution:** Components: $u_x = 20\cos 30° = 10\sqrt{3}$, $u_y = 20\sin 30° = 10$. Time of flight: $T = \frac{2u_y}{g} = \frac{20}{10} = 2$ s. Max height: $H = \frac{u_y^2}{2g} = \frac{100}{20} = 5$ m. Range: $R = u_x \cdot T = 10\sqrt{3} \times 2 = 20\sqrt{3} \approx 34.6$ m.

**Key insight:** Decompose first, then use SUVAT vertically and constant velocity horizontally.

**Example 2: Projectile on an Inclined Plane**

**Setup:** A particle is projected at $u$ from the base of an inclined plane at angle $\alpha$ to the horizontal, at angle $\theta$ to the plane. Find the range up the plane.

**Solution:** Resolve along and perpendicular to the plane: $u_{x'} = u\cos\theta$, $u_{y'} = u\sin\theta$. Accelerations: $a_{x'} = -g\sin\alpha$, $a_{y'} = -g\cos\alpha$. Time of flight (set $y' = 0$): $T = \frac{2u\sin\theta}{g\cos\alpha}$. Range: $R = u_{x'}T + \frac{1}{2}a_{x'}T^2 = \frac{u^2 \sin 2(\theta - \alpha)}{g \cos \alpha}$.

**Key insight:** On an incline, the optimal angle for maximum range up the plane is $\theta = 45° + \alpha/2$, not 45°.

**Example 3: Projectile Passing Through a Point**

**Setup:** A projectile is launched at $u = 25$ m/s. Does it pass through the point $(30, 10)$ m?

**Solution:** Substitute into trajectory: $10 = 30\tan\theta - \frac{10 \times 900}{2 \times 625 \cos^2\theta}$. Use $\sec^2\theta = 1 + \tan^2\theta$: $10 = 30\tan\theta - \frac{900}{1250}(1 + \tan^2\theta)$. Rearrange: $0.72\tan^2\theta - 30\tan\theta + 10.72 = 0$. Discriminant: $\Delta = 900 - 4(0.72)(10.72) = 900 - 30.9 = 869.1 > 0$. Two real solutions for $\tan\theta$ exist, so the point is reachable at two different angles.

**Key insight:** The discriminant being positive means the point is inside the "parabola of safety" — the boundary of all reachable points.
