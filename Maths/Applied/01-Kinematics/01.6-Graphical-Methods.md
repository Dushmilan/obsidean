
## Definition

Three motion graphs encode physics through **gradients** and **areas**:

| Graph | Gradient = | Area = |
|-------|-----------|--------|
| $s$–$t$ | velocity $v = ds/dt$ | — |
| $v$–$t$ | acceleration $a = dv/dt$ | displacement $s = \int v\,dt$ |
| $a$–$t$ | jerk $j = da/dt$ | change in velocity $\Delta v = \int a\,dt$ |

## The Intuition

A speedometer ($v$–$t$) and odometer ($s$–$t$): the odometer reading at any moment is the total area under the speedometer curve up to that time. The $s$–$t$ graph is a bird's-eye view of the road; the $v$–$t$ graph watches the speedometer needle; the $a$–$t$ graph feels the push in the seat. Gradients and areas are just calculus made visual.

## The Toolkit

| Feature | Reading |
|---------|---------|
| $s$–$t$ straight line | constant velocity |
| $s$–$t$ curvature | acceleration (concave up → $a > 0$) |
| $v$–$t$ straight line | constant acceleration |
| $v$ crosses zero | **turning point** (direction change) |
| Displacement | *signed* area under $v$–$t$ (below axis = negative) |
| Distance | *total* area (all positive) — split at $v = 0$ crossings |
| Average velocity | total displacement ÷ total time |
| Average speed | total distance ÷ total time |

## Derivation

The relationships are the definitions $v = ds/dt$ and $a = dv/dt$ read backwards — the FTC says the integral of the rate is the total change. A straight $v$–$t$ segment makes displacement a trapezium; the area formulas come straight from geometry. [Full derivations: 01.6-Proofs-Graphical-Methods]

## Method

1. **Extract from graphs:** gradient → velocity/acceleration; area → displacement/$\Delta v$.
2. **Compute distance:** split the $v$–$t$ integral at every $v = 0$ crossing and add absolute areas.
3. **Solve meeting problems:** find graph intersections (equal positions/times).
4. **Sketch graphs:** work from the description — straight segments for constant $a$, curves otherwise.

## Worked Examples

**Setup:** A particle starts from rest, accelerates uniformly for 4 s, moves at constant velocity for 6 s, then decelerates uniformly to rest in 2 s. Sketch $v$–$t$.

**Solution:** $v$–$t$: triangle $(0,0)\to(4,v_{\max})$, rectangle $(4,v_{\max})\to(10,v_{\max})$, triangle $(10,v_{\max})\to(12,0)$.

**Key insight:** The three phases have slopes $+a$, $0$, $-a$ — a rising line, a flat line, a falling line.

---

**Setup:** From a $v$–$t$ graph, a particle goes 5 m/s forward for 4 s, then 3 m/s backward for 2 s. Find displacement and distance.

**Solution:** Displacement $= 5\cdot4 + (-3)\cdot2 = 14$ m. Distance $= 20 + 6 = 26$ m.

**Key insight:** Displacement is signed area; distance is absolute area — split at the $v = 0$ crossing.

## Common Traps

- Counting below-axis $v$–$t$ area as positive for displacement
- Using the $s$–$t$ gradient where the $v$–$t$ gradient is needed
- Forgetting the $s$–$t$ graph must be continuous (position can't jump)
- Average speed vs average velocity — different when direction changes
- Reading "steeper" as "faster" on the wrong graph
