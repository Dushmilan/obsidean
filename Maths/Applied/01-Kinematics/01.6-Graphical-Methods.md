# Graphical Methods (Motion Graphs)

Three graphs (s-t, v-t, a-t) that represent motion visually, where gradients and areas encode physical quantities. Many kinematics problems are solved faster by reading gradients and areas than by algebra. Graphs also provide intuition that equations alone cannot.

**The Intuition:** Think of a speedometer (v-t) and an odometer (s-t). The speedometer needle moves up and down as you speed up and slow down. The odometer only goes up (or stays flat if you stop). The odometer reading at any moment is the total area under the speedometer curve up to that time.

An s-t graph is like a bird's-eye view of a car on a straight road. A v-t graph is like watching the speedometer needle over time. An a-t graph is like feeling the push back into your seat (acceleration) over time. These relationships follow directly from the definitions of velocity and acceleration as derivatives — gradients and areas are just calculus made visual.

**The Math:** The three fundamental relationships for rectilinear motion:

**Displacement-time (s-t) graph:** Gradient = velocity: $v = ds/dt$. Straight line means constant velocity. Curved means changing velocity (acceleration). Concavity tells you the sign of acceleration — concave up means positive acceleration, concave down means negative.

**Velocity-time (v-t) graph:** Gradient = acceleration: $a = dv/dt$. Area under curve = displacement: $s = \int v\,dt$. Straight line means constant acceleration. When $v$ crosses the $t$-axis ($v = 0$), the particle changes direction — this is a turning point.

**Acceleration-time (a-t) graph:** Area under curve = change in velocity: $\Delta v = \int a\,dt$. The gradient is jerk ($j = da/dt$), rarely used at A-level but important in engineering.

The critical distinction: displacement = signed area under v-t (areas below the axis count as negative), while distance = total area (all areas counted as positive). If $v$ crosses zero, you must split the integral at the zero crossing to compute distance. Average velocity = (total displacement)/(total time). Average speed = (total distance)/(total time). These are different when the particle changes direction.

**What does this mean for mechanics?** The s-t graph must always be continuous. The v-t graph must be continuous (unless infinite acceleration, which is unphysical). The a-t graph can have jumps. The key skills are reading a graph to extract physical information, sketching graphs from a verbal description, and using graphs to solve meeting/collision problems by finding intersection points.

**Worked Examples:**

**Example 1: Sketching Graphs from Description**

**Setup:** "Particle starts from rest, accelerates uniformly for 4 s, then moves at constant velocity for 6 s, then decelerates uniformly to rest in 2 s." Sketch s-t, v-t, and a-t graphs.

**Solution:** v-t: Triangle from $(0,0)$ to $(4, v_{\max})$, rectangle from $(4, v_{\max})$ to $(10, v_{\max})$, triangle from $(10, v_{\max})$ to $(12, 0)$. a-t: Horizontal line at $a_1$ from 0 to 4, $a = 0$ from 4 to 10, horizontal line at $a_2$ from 10 to 12. s-t: Parabola (concave up) from 0 to 4, straight line from 4 to 10, parabola (concave down) from 10 to 12.

**Key insight:** The s-t graph is always continuous and smooth (no kinks) because velocity is finite. The a-t graph can have jumps (discontinuous acceleration at transition points).

**Example 2: Distance vs Displacement from v-t**

**Setup:** $v = t^2 - 4t$ for $t$ in $[0, 4]$. Find (a) displacement, (b) distance travelled.

**Solution:** Find where $v = 0$: $t(t - 4) = 0$, so $t = 0$ and $t = 4$. $v$ is negative throughout $(0, 4)$. Displacement: $\int_0^4 (t^2 - 4t)\,dt = [t^3/3 - 2t^2]_0^4 = 64/3 - 32 = -32/3$. Distance: $\int_0^4 |t^2 - 4t|\,dt = \int_0^4 (4t - t^2)\,dt = [2t^2 - t^3/3]_0^4 = 32 - 64/3 = 32/3$.

**Key insight:** Displacement and distance have the same magnitude here because $v$ does not change sign. If $v$ crossed zero, they would differ.

**Example 3: Graphical Solution of Meeting Problem**

**Setup:** Particle A starts from rest with $a = 2$ m/s$^2$. Particle B starts 3 s later from the same point with $u = 0$ and $a = 4$ m/s$^2$. When do they meet?

**Solution:** Position of A: $s_A = t^2$. Position of B (for $t \geq 3$): $s_B = 2(t-3)^2$. Meet when $s_A = s_B$: $t^2 = 2(t-3)^2$. Solve: $t^2 - 12t + 18 = 0$. $t = 6 \pm 3\sqrt{2}$. $t = 6 - 4.24 = 1.76$ (before B starts, reject) or $t = 6 + 4.24 = 10.24$ s.

**Key insight:** The graphical approach shows that A is always ahead until B catches up. The intersection of the s-t curves gives the meeting time.
