Simple Harmonic Motion is defined by the differential equation $\ddot{x} + \omega^2 x = 0$, where $x$ is displacement from equilibrium and $\omega$ is the angular frequency (a constant determined by the system). The general solution is $x(t) = A\cos(\omega t + \phi)$, where $A$ is the amplitude and $\phi$ is the phase constant set by initial conditions. The defining characteristic is that acceleration is always proportional to displacement and directed opposite to it: $a = -\omega^2 x$. This is equivalent to a restoring force of the form $F = -kx$ (Hooke's law), a quadratic potential energy $U = \frac{1}{2}kx^2$, or the differential equation $\ddot{x} + \omega^2 x = 0$.

**The Intuition:** A mass on a spring — pull it aside and release. The spring pulls it back toward equilibrium. The further you pull, the harder it pulls back. The mass overshoots, the spring pulls it back the other way, and the cycle repeats. In phase space ($x$ vs $v$), SHM traces an ellipse. The particle cycles through: maximum displacement (zero velocity) $\to$ equilibrium (maximum velocity) $\to$ opposite maximum displacement (zero velocity) $\to$ equilibrium (maximum velocity) $\to$ back to start. A linear restoring force ($F = -kx$) produces acceleration proportional to displacement. The solution must be sinusoidal because only sinusoidal functions have derivatives that are proportional to themselves (up to sign).

**The Math:** From $F = -kx$ and Newton's 2nd law: $m\ddot{x} = -kx \implies \ddot{x} + (k/m)x = 0$. Identify $\omega^2 = k/m$. Solution: $x = A\cos(\omega t + \phi)$.

Velocity: $v = -A\omega\sin(\omega t + \phi) = \pm\omega\sqrt{A^2 - x^2}$. Acceleration: $a = -\omega^2 x$. Period: $T = 2\pi/\omega$. Frequency: $f = \omega/(2\pi)$.

Phase relationships: Velocity leads displacement by $\pi/2$. Acceleration leads velocity by $\pi/2$ (and displacement by $\pi$). Energy oscillates at $2\omega$: kinetic and potential energies each oscillate at twice the frequency of displacement.

Amplitude from initial conditions: $A = \sqrt{x_0^2 + (v_0/\omega)^2}$. Phase from initial conditions: $\phi = \arctan(-v_0/(\omega x_0))$ (check quadrant using signs of $x_0$ and $v_0$).

Five equivalent characterizations: (1) $a = -\omega^2 x$, (2) $F = -kx$, (3) $U = \frac{1}{2}kx^2$, (4) $\ddot{x} + \omega^2 x = 0$, (5) $x = A\cos(\omega t + \phi)$ with constant $\omega$.

**What does this mean for Applied Mechanics?** To prove SHM, derive the equation of motion and show it reduces to $\ddot{x} + \omega^2 x = 0$. Identify $\omega$ from the system parameters, then $T = 2\pi/\omega$. Use initial conditions to find $A$ and $\phi$. Remember: $v$ leads $x$ by $\pi/2$; $a$ is $\pi$ out of phase with $x$. For the simple pendulum, the small-angle approximation is required for SHM.

**Setup:** A particle of mass $m$ hangs from a spring of natural length $l$ and modulus of elasticity $\lambda$. It hangs vertically in equilibrium, is displaced vertically, and released. Show the motion is SHM and find the period.

**Solution:** At equilibrium: $kx_0 = mg$ where $k = \lambda/l$. Displace by $y$ from equilibrium: net force $= mg - k(x_0 + y) = -ky$. So $m\ddot{y} = -ky \implies \ddot{y} + (k/m)y = 0$. This is SHM with $\omega = \sqrt{k/m} = \sqrt{\lambda/(ml)}$. Period: $T = 2\pi\sqrt{ml/\lambda}$.

**Key insight:** Gravity shifts the equilibrium but does not affect $\omega$ or $T$.

**Setup:** SHM with $\omega = 4$ rad/s. At $t = 0$, $x = 0.03$ m and $v = -0.12$ m/s. Find $A$ and $\phi$.

**Solution:** $A = \sqrt{x_0^2 + (v_0/\omega)^2} = \sqrt{0.03^2 + (-0.12/4)^2} = \sqrt{0.0009 + 0.0009} = 0.03\sqrt{2} \approx 0.0424$ m. $\phi = \arctan(-v_0/(\omega x_0)) = \arctan(0.12/(4 \times 0.03)) = \arctan(1) = \pi/4$. Check quadrant: $x_0 > 0$ and $v_0 < 0$, so $\phi = \pi/4$ is correct.

**Key insight:** The phase constant quadrant depends on the signs of both $x_0$ and $v_0$.
