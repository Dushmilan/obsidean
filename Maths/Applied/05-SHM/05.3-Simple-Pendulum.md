A simple pendulum consists of a point mass $m$ (bob) suspended from a light, inextensible string of length $L$ by a frictionless pivot. The exact equation of motion is $\ddot{\theta} + (g/L)\sin\theta = 0$, which is nonlinear. For small oscillations ($\theta \lesssim 10°$), the approximation $\sin\theta \approx \theta$ linearises the equation to $\ddot{\theta} + (g/L)\theta = 0$, which is SHM with $\omega = \sqrt{g/L}$ and $T = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$. The period is independent of mass $m$ and (for small angles) independent of amplitude $\theta_0$ — the isochronous property.

**The Intuition:** A child on a swing — push gently and it swings back and forth with a regular rhythm. The period depends on the chain length, not the child's weight. At the bottom of the swing, all energy is kinetic. At the extremes, all energy is potential. The exchange between kinetic and potential energy drives the oscillation, with gravity providing the restoring torque. A longer string means the bob travels a longer arc for the same angle, taking more time. A stronger gravitational field means a stronger restoring torque, shortening the period. Hence $T \propto \sqrt{L/g}$.

**The Math:** Torque about pivot: $\tau = -mgL\sin\theta$. Newton's 2nd law for rotation: $I\ddot{\theta} = \tau$ where $I = mL^2$. So $\ddot{\theta} + (g/L)\sin\theta = 0$. Small angle: $\ddot{\theta} + (g/L)\theta = 0$.

Angular frequency (small angle): $\omega = \sqrt{g/L}$. Period (small angle): $T = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$.

Large-angle correction: $T \approx T_0(1 + \theta_0^2/16)$ for moderate angles. The period increases with amplitude — the pendulum is no longer isochronous.

Physical (compound) pendulum: $T = 2\pi\sqrt{I_O/(Mgd)}$, where $I_O$ is the moment of inertia about the pivot and $d$ is the distance from pivot to centre of mass. Equivalent length: $L_{\text{eq}} = I_O/(Md) = k^2/h + h$.

Speed at angle $\theta$: $v = \sqrt{2gL(\cos\theta - \cos\theta_0)}$. Potential energy: $U = mgL(1-\cos\theta) \approx \frac{1}{2}mgL\theta^2$.

**What does this mean for Applied Mechanics?** Check the amplitude: if $\theta_0 < 10°$, use $T = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$ directly. For large angles, apply the correction. For physical pendulums, find $I_O$ using parallel axis theorem, then $T = 2\pi\sqrt{I_O/(Mgd)}$. To measure $g$, rearrange: $g = 4\pi^2 L/T^2$. Remember: conical pendulum period $= T_0\sqrt{\cos\theta} < T_0$.

**Setup:** Pendulum length $L = 1$ m. Find period on Earth ($g = 9.8$ m/s²) and Moon ($g = 1.6$ m/s²).

**Solution:** $T_{\text{Earth}} = 2\pi\sqrt{1/9.8} \approx 2.01$ s. $T_{\text{Moon}} = 2\pi\sqrt{1/1.6} \approx 4.97$ s.

**Key insight:** The pendulum swings about 2.5 times slower on the Moon.

**Setup:** $L = 1$ m, amplitude $\theta_0 = 30°$. Find the period.

**Solution:** $\theta_0 = 30° = \pi/6 \approx 0.524$ rad. $T \approx T_0(1 + \theta_0^2/16) = 2.01(1 + 0.524^2/16) \approx 2.01(1.017) \approx 2.04$ s.

**Key insight:** The 30° correction is only 1.7% — the small-angle approximation is surprisingly robust.
