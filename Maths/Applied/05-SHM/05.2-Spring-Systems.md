A spring-mass system consists of a mass $m$ attached to a spring with spring constant $k$. Hooke's law gives the restoring force $F = -kx$, where $x$ is displacement from the natural length (for horizontal) or from equilibrium (for vertical). The system undergoes SHM with angular frequency $\omega = \sqrt{k/m}$ and period $T = 2\pi\sqrt{m/k}$. The key insight for vertical springs is that gravity shifts the equilibrium position but does not change the oscillation frequency. The equilibrium extension is $x_0 = mg/k$, and displacement $y$ from this equilibrium gives the same equation of motion $m\ddot{y} = -ky$ as the horizontal case.

**The Intuition:** A trampoline — push down and it pushes back proportionally. The harder you push (larger displacement), the stronger the restoring force. For a vertical spring, the mass hangs at equilibrium where $kx_0 = mg$. Displacing it by $y$ from this point gives a net force $-ky$, identical to the horizontal case. Gravity is "already accounted for" by the equilibrium extension. In series, the same force flows through both springs, so the softer spring dominates. In parallel, both springs share the displacement, so the stiffer spring dominates.

**The Math:** From $F = -kx$: $m\ddot{x} = -kx \implies \ddot{x} + (k/m)x = 0 \implies \omega = \sqrt{k/m}$.

Period: $T = 2\pi\sqrt{m/k}$. Frequency: $f = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{k/m}$. Equilibrium extension (vertical): $x_0 = mg/k$.

Series combination: $1/k_{\text{eq}} = 1/k_1 + 1/k_2$ (like resistors in parallel). Parallel combination: $k_{\text{eq}} = k_1 + k_2$ (like resistors in series). Do not confuse the two.

Massive spring adds $\frac{1}{3}m_s$: The spring's own mass contributes to inertia. The effective oscillating mass is $m_{\text{load}} + \frac{1}{3}m_{\text{spring}}$. This is because different parts of the spring move at different speeds.

Two bodies ($m_1, m_2$) on spring: Use reduced mass $\mu = m_1 m_2/(m_1 + m_2)$ in the frequency formula: $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\mu/k}$.

**What does this mean for Applied Mechanics?** For vertical springs, find the equilibrium extension first, then measure displacement from equilibrium. For series/parallel combinations, compute $k_{\text{eq}}$ first, then use $T = 2\pi\sqrt{m/k_{\text{eq}}}$. For massive springs, add $\frac{1}{3}m_s$ to the load mass. Remember: $k = \lambda/l$ — do not confuse spring constant with modulus of elasticity.

**Setup:** Three identical springs ($k = 100$ N/m). Two in parallel, then in series with the third. $m = 2$ kg. Find $k_{\text{eq}}$ and $T$.

**Solution:** $k_{12} = k + k = 200$ N/m (parallel). $1/k_{\text{eq}} = 1/200 + 1/100 = 3/200 \implies k_{\text{eq}} = 200/3 \approx 66.67$ N/m. $T = 2\pi\sqrt{2/66.67} \approx 2\pi\sqrt{0.03} \approx 1.09$ s.

**Key insight:** The parallel combination doubles the stiffness, but the series connection with the third spring reduces the overall stiffness.

**Setup:** $m_1 = 2$ kg, $m_2 = 3$ kg, connected by spring $k = 150$ N/m on a smooth table. Find the oscillation period.

**Solution:** $\mu = (2 \times 3)/(2 + 3) = 1.2$ kg. $T = 2\pi\sqrt{1.2/150} = 2\pi\sqrt{0.008} \approx 0.56$ s.

**Key insight:** The reduced mass $\mu$ is always less than the lighter mass — the system oscillates faster than either mass alone.
