In SHM, the total mechanical energy is conserved and equals the maximum potential energy or maximum kinetic energy: $E = \frac{1}{2}kA^2$. The kinetic energy $K = \frac{1}{2}k(A^2 - x^2)$ and potential energy $U = \frac{1}{2}kx^2$ exchange continuously: at maximum displacement ($x = \pm A$), all energy is potential; at equilibrium ($x = 0$), all energy is kinetic. A key observation is that $K$ and $U$ each oscillate at twice the frequency of displacement: $\omega_K = 2\omega$. When $x$ completes one cycle, $K$ and $U$ each complete two cycles. They are out of phase by $\pi$: when one is maximum, the other is minimum.

**The Intuition:** A ball rolling in a bowl — at the rim, it has maximum potential energy and zero kinetic energy. At the bottom, it has maximum kinetic energy and minimum potential energy. The total energy is constant (no friction). In the $x$-$v$ phase space, the trajectory is an ellipse. The area inside the ellipse is proportional to the total energy: Area $= \pi A \cdot A\omega = \pi A^2\omega = 2\pi E/\omega$. A conservative force (like a spring) conserves mechanical energy. Since the potential is quadratic ($U = \frac{1}{2}kx^2$), the energy exchange is sinusoidal, and the total is constant.

**The Math:** Total energy: $E = \frac{1}{2}kA^2 = \frac{1}{2}mv_{\max}^2$. Kinetic energy: $K = \frac{1}{2}k(A^2 - x^2)$. Potential energy: $U = \frac{1}{2}kx^2$. Velocity at displacement $x$: $v = \pm\omega\sqrt{A^2 - x^2}$.

Energy method for finding $\omega$: expand the potential energy $U(x)$ about a stable equilibrium $x_0$ to second order: $U(x) \approx U(x_0) + \frac{1}{2}U''(x_0)(x-x_0)^2$. Then $k_{\text{eff}} = U''(x_0)$ and $\omega = \sqrt{k_{\text{eff}}/m}$. This works for any conservative 1D system, not just springs.

Phase space: $x^2/A^2 + v^2/(A\omega)^2 = 1$ (ellipse). Phase space area $= \pi A^2\omega = 2\pi E/\omega$.

Doubling the amplitude quadruples the energy. This is why large-amplitude oscillations are energetically expensive. For vertical springs, the constant term in $U$ does not affect $\omega$ — only the quadratic term matters. Energy conservation is not valid for damped systems (energy decays exponentially).

**What does this mean for Applied Mechanics?** Total energy $E = \frac{1}{2}kA^2$ is the easiest way to find amplitude from energy (or vice versa). For the energy method, expand $U(x)$ about equilibrium to second order; the coefficient of $\frac{1}{2}(x-x_0)^2$ is $k_{\text{eff}}$. Remember that $K$ and $U$ oscillate at $2\omega$. Energy conservation is the fastest route for finding speed at a given position.

**Setup:** $m = 0.5$ kg, $k = 200$ N/m, $A = 0.1$ m. Find $K$ and $U$ when $x = 0.05$ m.

**Solution:** $E = \frac{1}{2}kA^2 = \frac{1}{2}(200)(0.1)^2 = 1$ J. $U = \frac{1}{2}kx^2 = \frac{1}{2}(200)(0.05)^2 = 0.25$ J. $K = E - U = 0.75$ J. $K/E = 75\%$, $U/E = 25\%$.

**Key insight:** At half the amplitude, the energy is 75% kinetic and 25% potential (not 50/50).

**Setup:** Simple pendulum, length $L$, mass $m$, amplitude $\theta_0$. Find speed at angle $\theta$.

**Solution:** Total energy: $E = mgL(1-\cos\theta_0)$. At angle $\theta$: $E = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 + mgL(1-\cos\theta)$. $\frac{1}{2}mv^2 = mgL(\cos\theta - \cos\theta_0)$. $v = \sqrt{2gL(\cos\theta - \cos\theta_0)}$.

**Key insight:** Energy conservation avoids the need to solve the equation of motion directly.
