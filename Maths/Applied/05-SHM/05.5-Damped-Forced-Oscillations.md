Real oscillators experience damping — a resistive force proportional to velocity ($F_d = -b\dot{x}$) that dissipates energy. The equation of motion becomes $m\ddot{x} + b\dot{x} + kx = 0$, or $\ddot{x} + 2\gamma\dot{x} + \omega_0^2 x = 0$, where $\gamma = b/(2m)$ is the damping constant and $\omega_0 = \sqrt{k/m}$ is the natural frequency. Three regimes exist depending on $\gamma$ relative to $\omega_0$: underdamped ($\gamma < \omega_0$, decaying oscillations), critically damped ($\gamma = \omega_0$, fastest return without oscillation), and overdamped ($\gamma > \omega_0$, slow exponential decay). The quality factor $Q = \omega_0/(2\gamma)$ quantifies damping severity.

**The Intuition:** A swing in air — air resistance gradually reduces the amplitude (damping). If someone pushes at the right frequency (forcing), the swing builds up to a large amplitude (resonance). Damping is like friction that steals energy each cycle. The energy decays as $E(t) \approx E_0 e^{-2\gamma t}$, and the amplitude as $A(t) = A_0 e^{-\gamma t}$. Forcing replenishes the stolen energy — at resonance, the energy input rate matches the dissipation rate. The resonance frequency is slightly below $\omega_0$ because damping shifts the peak. At resonance, the driving force is always in phase with the velocity (not the displacement), maximising power transfer.

**The Math:** Characteristic equation $r^2 + 2\gamma r + \omega_0^2 = 0$ gives roots $r = -\gamma \pm \sqrt{\gamma^2 - \omega_0^2}$. Underdamped: $x = Ae^{-\gamma t}\cos(\omega_d t + \phi)$ with $\omega_d = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \gamma^2}$.

Critical damping: $b_c = 2\sqrt{mk}$. Quality factor: $Q = \omega_0/(2\gamma)$. Logarithmic decrement: $\delta = \ln(A_n/A_{n+1}) = \pi/Q$.

Forced steady state: $A(\omega) = \frac{F_0/m}{\sqrt{(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)^2 + 4\gamma^2\omega^2}}$. Phase lag: $\tan\phi = 2\gamma\omega/(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)$.

Resonance frequency: $\omega_r = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - 2\gamma^2}$. Max amplitude at resonance (light damping): $A_{\max} \approx QF_0/(m\omega_0^2)$. Half-power bandwidth: $\Delta\omega \approx 2\gamma = \omega_0/Q$.

Critical damping is the fastest return without oscillation — overdamping is slower. Power resonance is at $\omega_0$, not $\omega_r$ (the amplitude peaks at $\omega_r$, but the power absorbed peaks at exactly $\omega_0$). Phase at resonance is $\pi/2$: the displacement lags the driving force by a quarter cycle, so the velocity is in phase with the force.

**What does this mean for Applied Mechanics?** Classify damping by comparing $\gamma = b/(2m)$ with $\omega_0 = \sqrt{k/m}$. For underdamped systems, use $\omega_d = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \gamma^2}$ (not $\omega_0$) for the oscillation frequency. For resonance problems, check whether the question asks for amplitude resonance ($\omega_r$) or power resonance ($\omega_0$). The Q-factor is the single most useful parameter: it tells you damping severity, bandwidth, and number of oscillations.

**Setup:** $m = 0.5$ kg, $k = 200$ N/m, $b = 2$ N$\cdot$s/m. Classify the damping and find $\omega_d$ and $Q$.

**Solution:** $\omega_0 = \sqrt{200/0.5} = 20$ rad/s, $\gamma = b/(2m) = 2/(2 \times 0.5) = 2$ s$^{-1}$. $\gamma = 2 < \omega_0 = 20$ — underdamped. $\omega_d = \sqrt{20^2 - 2^2} = \sqrt{396} \approx 19.9$ rad/s. $Q = \omega_0/(2\gamma) = 20/4 = 5$.

**Key insight:** $Q = 5$ means the amplitude decays to $e^{-\pi} \approx 1/23$ of its initial value after about $5/\pi \approx 1.6$ oscillations.

**Setup:** $m = 2$ kg, $k = 50$ N/m. Find the damping coefficient $b$ for critical damping.

**Solution:** $\gamma = \omega_0 \implies b/(2m) = \sqrt{k/m} \implies b = 2\sqrt{mk} = 2\sqrt{2 \times 50} = 20$ N$\cdot$s/m.

**Key insight:** Critical damping gives the fastest return to equilibrium without overshooting — used in door closers and galvanometers.
