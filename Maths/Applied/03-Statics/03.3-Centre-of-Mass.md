The centre of mass is the bridge between particle mechanics and rigid body mechanics. When you replace a distributed body with a single point mass at the CM, translational equations become identical to particle equations. The CM is also the point about which rotation naturally occurs. For composite bodies (L-shapes, bodies with holes), the subtraction method is essential: treat the hole as negative mass. This avoids integration and reduces problems to algebra with standard results.

**The Intuition:** The CM is the balancing point. If you could support the entire body at one point, the CM is where it would balance perfectly — like balancing a plate on your finger. For a uniform triangle, the CM is at the centroid (intersection of medians) — the "average position" of all the material. Each piece of mass contributes proportionally to its distance from the origin. The CM is the weighted average position.

**The Math:** Discrete CM: $\vec{r}_{\text{cm}} = \frac{\sum m_i \vec{r}_i}{M}$. Continuous CM: $\vec{r}_{\text{cm}} = \frac{1}{M}\int \vec{r}\,dm$.

Subtraction method: $\vec{r}_{\text{cm}} = \frac{m_{\text{full}}\vec{r}_{\text{full}} - m_{\text{hole}}\vec{r}_{\text{hole}}}{m_{\text{total}}}$.

Standard results: Semicircular lamina: $y_{\text{cm}} = \frac{4r}{3\pi}$ from diameter. Solid hemisphere: $z_{\text{cm}} = \frac{3r}{8}$ from base. Solid cone: $z_{\text{cm}} = \frac{h}{4}$ from base.

Pappus surface area: $A = s \cdot 2\pi\bar{y}$. Pappus volume: $V = A \cdot 2\pi\bar{y}$.

The CM does not have to be inside the physical body — a ring's CM is at its centre (empty space). When removing material (holes, cutouts), treat the removed part as negative mass. If a body has a plane of symmetry, the CM lies on that plane. If it has two planes of symmetry, the CM lies on their intersection line. Always check for symmetry before calculating.

**What does this mean for Applied Mechanics?** CM determines where weight acts in equilibrium problems, whether a body topples, and is the reference point for virtual work. The subtraction method is your most powerful tool for exam problems. Memorise the standard results and always check for symmetry — it saves time and prevents errors. The CM may lie outside the body (e.g., ring, hollow sphere).

**Setup:** Uniform lamina: $10\text{cm} \times 10\text{cm}$ square with $4\text{cm} \times 4\text{cm}$ removed from one corner. Find CM from two adjacent sides of original square.

**Solution:** Full square: $m_1 = 100\rho$, CM at $(5,5)$. Removed square: $m_2 = -16\rho$, CM at $(8,8)$. Total mass: $84\rho$. $x_{\text{cm}} = \frac{100(5) - 16(8)}{84} = \frac{500 - 128}{84} = \frac{372}{84} = \frac{31}{7} \approx 4.43$ cm. By symmetry, $y_{\text{cm}} = \frac{31}{7}$ cm.

**Key insight:** The subtraction method avoids integration entirely. Treat the hole as negative mass and apply the discrete formula.

**Setup:** Rectangular lamina $a \times b$ with circular hole radius $r$ at position $(x_0, y_0)$ from centre. Find CM.

**Solution:** Rectangle: $m_1 = ab\rho$, CM at $(0,0)$. Hole: $m_2 = -\pi r^2\rho$, CM at $(x_0, y_0)$. $x_{\text{cm}} = \frac{-\pi r^2 x_0}{ab - \pi r^2}$, $y_{\text{cm}} = \frac{-\pi r^2 y_0}{ab - \pi r^2}$.

**Key insight:** The CM shifts away from the hole — towards the material that remains.
