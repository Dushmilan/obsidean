
## Definition

Friction is **self-adjusting**: it grows to match the applied force up to a maximum.

- **Static friction:** holds a body still — adjusts $0 \le F \le \mu R$.
- **Limiting friction:** the threshold $F = \mu R$ (about to slide).
- **Kinetic friction:** acts once sliding begins ($F = \mu_k R$).
- **Angle of friction:** $\tan\lambda = \mu$ — the resultant contact force tilts by $\lambda$ from the normal at limiting equilibrium.

## The Intuition

A bouncer at a door: lets small forces in up to a capacity, then blocks. The capacity is $\mu R$ — more weight pressing down means more grip. Pull a block gently and friction matches; pull harder and it grows; at some point the block slips — that threshold is $\mu R$. Surface roughness creates microscopic interlocking; more normal force presses the surfaces together harder.

## The Toolkit

| Result | Formula | Valid when |
|--------|---------|-----------|
| Limiting friction | $F = \mu R$ | at the threshold of sliding |
| Angle of friction | $\tan\lambda = \mu$ | always |
| Equilibrium on incline | $W(\sin\theta - \mu\cos\theta) \le P \le W(\sin\theta + \mu\cos\theta)$ | applied along slope |
| Capstan equation | $\dfrac{T_2}{T_1} = e^{\mu\theta}$ | rope over rough peg ($\theta$ in radians) |
| Sliding angle | $\theta = \tan^{-1}\mu$ | incline that just slips |

## Derivation

The limiting condition $F = \mu R$ is empirical (Coulomb). The incline range comes from force balance at the two limiting equilibria: about to slide up ($P = W\sin\theta + \mu W\cos\theta$) and about to slide down ($P = W\sin\theta - \mu W\cos\theta$). The capstan equation: tension in a rope element over angle $d\theta$ balances the friction increment — integrate to get the exponential. [Full derivations: 03.2-Proofs-Friction]

## Method

1. **Ask: "which way would the body move if there were no friction?"** Friction opposes that tendency.
2. Determine whether the body is at limiting equilibrium: $F = \mu R$ only *at the threshold*; otherwise $F < \mu R$ (an unknown, found from force balance).
3. For motion problems, test both failure modes (see 03.4-Toppling-and-Sliding).

## Worked Examples

**Setup:** A 50 N block on a horizontal floor, $\mu = 0.4$. A 15 N horizontal force is applied. Does it move?

**Solution:** $R = 50$ N, limiting $F = \mu R = 20$ N. Since $15 < 20$, static friction matches at 15 N — it stays put.

**Key insight:** Static friction adjusts; $F = \mu R$ only holds at the limit. Don't set friction equal to $\mu R$ when the body is at rest.

---

**Setup:** A block of weight $W$ just slides down an incline at $30^\circ$. Find $\mu$.

**Solution:** At limiting equilibrium: $W\sin30 = \mu W\cos30 \Rightarrow \mu = \tan30 = 1/\sqrt3$.

**Key insight:** The sliding angle is $\tan^{-1}\mu$ — one measurement gives the coefficient.

## Common Traps

- Using $F = \mu R$ when the body is not about to move
- Friction opposing *actual* motion rather than the *tendency* to move
- Forgetting the normal reaction changes when forces are applied at angles (not always $mg$)
- Capstan equation needs $\theta$ in radians
- Kinetic vs static coefficients — they differ; use the right one once sliding
