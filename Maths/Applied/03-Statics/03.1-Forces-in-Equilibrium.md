Forces in equilibrium is the foundation of all statics. If a body is not accelerating and not rotating, three conditions must hold simultaneously: $\sum F_x = 0$, $\sum F_y = 0$, $\sum M_O = 0$. In 2D, you always get exactly three equations — two from force resolution and one from moments — which means you can solve for up to three unknowns. The moment equation is what separates particle mechanics from rigid body mechanics. Without it, you cannot handle problems where forces act at different points on a body.

**The Intuition:** Imagine holding a rod at its centre. If someone pushes at one end and you push at the other with equal force, the rod stays put (force balance). But if one of you pushes higher up and the other pushes lower down, the rod might stay still but start rotating — so you also need moment balance. Three conditions: left-right, up-down, and no-spin. All three must be satisfied simultaneously. A single force can be balanced by an opposite force, but if those forces do not share the same line of action, they create a couple. Moments account for this rotation tendency.

**The Math:** A rigid body is in equilibrium when both translational and rotational equilibrium hold: $\sum \vec{F} = 0$ and $\sum \vec{M}_O = 0$.

Lami's Theorem (three forces at a single point): $\frac{F_1}{\sin\alpha_1} = \frac{F_2}{\sin\alpha_2} = \frac{F_3}{\sin\alpha_3}$. This comes from the sine rule applied to the triangle of forces.

Principle of Moments: Sum of clockwise moments = sum of anticlockwise moments. Moment of a force: $M = F \cdot d_{\perp}$ where $d_{\perp}$ is the perpendicular distance from pivot to line of action.

Two forces can only be in equilibrium if they are equal in magnitude, opposite in direction, and collinear. For three forces at a point, Lami's theorem replaces the resolution method with a direct ratio. For more forces, use resolution. Always choose your moment point where two or more unknowns pass through — this eliminates them from the equation.

**What does this mean for Applied Mechanics?** Lami's theorem only works for exactly three forces at a point. For everything else, use resolution. Draw a full force diagram before writing any equations. Check your answer by taking moments about a different point — it should still balance. Always verify $F \leq \mu R$ after solving — the solution may give a friction force that exceeds the limit.

**Setup:** Uniform beam length $L$, weight $W$, supported at ends $A$ and $B$. Load $P$ at distance $x$ from left end. Find reactions $R_A$ and $R_B$.

**Solution:** $\sum F_y = R_A + R_B - W - P = 0$. $\sum M_A = R_B L - W(L/2) - Px = 0$. Solve: $R_B = \frac{W}{2} + \frac{Px}{L}$, $R_A = W + P - R_B = \frac{W}{2} + \frac{P(L-x)}{L}$.

**Key insight:** Taking moments about $A$ eliminates $R_A$ from the equation, giving $R_B$ directly.

**Setup:** Uniform ladder length $L$, weight $W$, angle $\alpha$ to horizontal. Rough floor ($\mu$), smooth wall. Find the friction force and normal reactions.

**Solution:** $\sum F_x = F_f - R_w = 0 \Rightarrow F_f = R_w$. $\sum F_y = R_f - W = 0 \Rightarrow R_f = W$. $\sum M_{\text{bottom}} = R_w L\sin\alpha - W(L/2)\cos\alpha = 0 \Rightarrow R_w = \frac{W}{2}\cot\alpha$.

**Key insight:** The moment equation about the bottom eliminates both $R_f$ and $F_f$, giving $R_w$ immediately.
