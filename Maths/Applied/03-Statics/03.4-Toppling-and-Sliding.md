Toppling and sliding are competing failure modes. When a force pushes a body, it can either slide (overcome friction) or topple (rotate about an edge). The critical comparison is between the friction coefficient $\mu$ and the aspect ratio $\frac{b}{2h}$. If friction is weak relative to the geometry, sliding wins. If the body is tall and narrow, toppling wins. The force application point matters enormously — pushing at the top makes toppling easier ($P_{\text{topple}} \propto 1/y$), while pushing at the bottom makes sliding easier.

**The Intuition:** Imagine pushing a tall wardrobe. If the floor is slippery (low $\mu$), it slides. If the floor is grippy (high $\mu$) but the wardrobe is tall and narrow, it topples. The wardrobe "wants" to rotate about its bottom edge when pushed high up. A box tipping over its lower edge: the critical moment is when the line of action of weight passes exactly through that edge. Beyond that, gravity pulls it over. Toppling requires the weight's line of action to fall outside the base. Sliding requires the applied force to exceed friction. These are independent conditions.

**The Math:** Sliding angle: $\theta_{\text{slide}} = \tan^{-1}\mu$. Toppling angle: $\theta_{\text{topple}} = \tan^{-1}\frac{b}{2h}$.

Sliding force (horizontal): $P_{\text{slide}} = \mu W$. Toppling force (horizontal at height $y$): $P_{\text{topple}} = \frac{Wb}{2y}$.

Compare $\mu$ and $\frac{b}{2h}$: If $\mu < \frac{b}{2h}$: slides first. If $\mu > \frac{b}{2h}$: topples first. If $\mu = \frac{b}{2h}$: simultaneous.

Lower force application $\rightarrow$ harder to topple ($P_{\text{topple}} \propto 1/y$). At $y = h$ (top edge): $P_{\text{topple}} = \frac{Wb}{2h}$. At $y = h/2$ (mid-height): $P_{\text{topple}} = \frac{Wb}{h}$. An upward force ($\alpha > 0$) reduces normal reaction $\rightarrow$ easier to slide, harder to topple. A downward force ($\alpha < 0$) increases normal reaction $\rightarrow$ harder to slide, easier to topple.

On an incline, gravity provides both a sliding force ($mg\sin\theta$) and a toppling moment (about the lower edge). The critical angles are $\tan^{-1}\mu$ for sliding and $\tan^{-1}(b/2h)$ for toppling.

**What does this mean for Applied Mechanics?** Always calculate both $\theta_{\text{slide}}$ and $\theta_{\text{topple}}$ and compare them. Check where the force is applied — height matters for toppling. For angled forces, resolve fully and recalculate the normal reaction. Draw the line of action of weight relative to the base edge to visualise toppling. Inclines with external forces require checking three conditions: slide up, slide down, topple.

**Setup:** Cube side $a$, mass $m$, $\mu = 0.5$. Incline angle $\theta$. Does it slide or topple first?

**Solution:** $\theta_{\text{slide}} = \tan^{-1}(0.5) \approx 26.6°$. $\theta_{\text{topple}} = \tan^{-1}(a/2a) = \tan^{-1}(0.5) \approx 26.6°$.

**Key insight:** For a cube, $b/2h = 1/2$, so if $\mu = 0.5$, both conditions are met simultaneously.

**Setup:** Block $b = 0.4$ m, $h = 0.6$ m, $W = 100$ N, $\mu = 0.3$. Force $P$ at $y = 0.5$ m. Does it slide or topple first?

**Solution:** $P_{\text{slide}} = \mu W = 0.3 \times 100 = 30$ N. $P_{\text{topple}} = \frac{Wb}{2y} = \frac{100 \times 0.4}{2 \times 0.5} = 40$ N. Slides first (at 30 N).

**Key insight:** The sliding threshold is lower, so sliding dominates. This happens because $\mu = 0.3 < b/2h = 0.33$.
