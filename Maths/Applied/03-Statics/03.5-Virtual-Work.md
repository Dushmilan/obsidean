Virtual work is an alternative to the standard equilibrium equations. Instead of resolving forces and taking moments, you imagine a small displacement consistent with the constraints and set the total work done to zero. The beauty is that constraint forces (which do no work in consistent displacements) vanish from the equation. This method is especially powerful for simple machines (levers, inclined planes, wedges, screw jacks) and for systems where finding constraint forces is tedious. A single virtual displacement gives one equation, which is exactly what you need for a one-degree-of-freedom system.

**The Intuition:** Imagine a lever in equilibrium. If you push it slightly, the work you do must equal the work done against the load. Since it is in equilibrium, the net work is zero — your push and the load's resistance cancel out. A seesaw balanced on a fulcrum: nudge it slightly — the child going down gains work, the child going up loses work. In equilibrium, these balance exactly. If a system is in equilibrium, any imagined displacement cannot change the energy. The total work must be zero for all possible displacements.

**The Math:** Principle of Virtual Work: $\delta W = \sum \vec{F}_i \cdot \delta\vec{r}_i = 0$ for equilibrium.

A virtual displacement $\delta\vec{r}$ is an infinitesimal change in position consistent with constraints, imposed instantaneously (time frozen). Constraint forces (normal reactions, tensions in inextensible strings, reactions at fixed pins) do no work in virtual displacements consistent with constraints because $\vec{R} \perp \delta\vec{r}$ or $\delta\vec{r} = 0$.

Simple machines: Lever: $Ea = Lb$. Inclined plane: $P = W\sin\theta$. Wedge: $P = W\tan\alpha$. Screw Jack: $E = \frac{Wp}{2\pi R}$.

D'Alembert's Principle: $\delta W = \sum (\vec{F}_i - m_i\vec{a}_i) \cdot \delta\vec{r}_i = 0$ (extends to dynamics by including inertial force $-m\vec{a}$ as a "force").

For one-DOF systems, choose rotation about a pivot or fixed point. This eliminates unknowns at that point (they do no work). The coefficient of $\delta\theta$ (or $\delta x$) must be zero since the displacement is arbitrary.

**What does this mean for Applied Mechanics?** Virtual work eliminates constraint forces, gives one equation per DOF, and handles complex geometry naturally. Master the art of choosing the right virtual displacement, and you will solve problems faster than with standard methods. For multi-DOF systems, use multiple independent virtual displacements.

**Setup:** Uniform ladder length $2l$, weight $W$, angle $\theta$. Smooth wall and floor. Find horizontal force $F$ at bottom to maintain equilibrium.

**Solution:** Virtual rotation $\delta\theta$ about bottom. CM rises: $\delta y = l\cos\theta\,\delta\theta$. Top moves horizontally: $\delta x = 2l\sin\theta\,\delta\theta$. Force $F$ does work $F\delta x$. $\delta W = F(2l\sin\theta\,\delta\theta) - W(l\cos\theta\,\delta\theta) = 0$. $F = \frac{W}{2}\cot\theta$.

**Key insight:** Choosing rotation about the bottom eliminates the floor reaction and the wall reaction (both do zero work in this displacement).

**Setup:** Two links length $l$, hinged at ends and centre. Force $P$ applied horizontally at centre. Vertical load $W$ at hinge between links. Angle between links = $2\theta$. Find $P$ for equilibrium.

**Solution:** Virtual displacement: $\theta \to \theta + \delta\theta$. Centre moves horizontally: $\delta x = l\sin\theta\,\delta\theta$. Load $W$ moves vertically: $\delta y = l\cos\theta\,\delta\theta$. $\delta W = P(l\sin\theta\,\delta\theta) - W(l\cos\theta\,\delta\theta) = 0$. $P = W\cot\theta$.

**Key insight:** As $\theta \to 0$ (links nearly straight), $P \to \infty$ — the toggle joint amplifies force enormously near the fully extended position.
