Moment of inertia (MI) measures how mass is distributed relative to an axis of rotation. For a system of discrete masses, $I = \sum m_i r_i^2$; for a continuous body, $I = \int r^2\,dm$. The radius of gyration $k = \sqrt{I/M}$ is the distance from the axis at which all the mass could be concentrated to give the same MI. Two fundamental theorems simplify MI calculations. The parallel axes theorem states $I = I_{CM} + Md^2$, where $I_{CM}$ is the MI about a parallel axis through the centre of mass and $d$ is the distance between axes. The perpendicular axes theorem states $I_z = I_x + I_y$ for a planar lamina lying in the $xy$-plane — it does not apply to 3D bodies.

**The Intuition:** Pushing a door — the MI depends only on mass distribution, not on the applied force. Mass far from the axis contributes a lot ($mr^2$); mass near the axis contributes little. The $r^2$ weighting means outer layers dominate. A hollow cylinder has more MI than a solid cylinder of the same mass, because its mass is concentrated farther from the axis. MI is minimum about the CM axis — any parallel axis gives a larger MI by $Md^2$.

**The Math:** Parallel axes theorem: $I = I_{CM} + Md^2$. Derivation: For a mass element $dm$ at position $\vec{r}$ from CM, its distance from a parallel axis at distance $d$ is $|\vec{r} + \vec{d}|$. Integrating: $I = \int(r^2 + d^2 + 2\vec{r}\cdot\vec{d})\,dm = I_{CM} + Md^2$ (since $\int \vec{r}\,dm = 0$ by CM definition).

Perpendicular axes theorem: $I_z = I_x + I_y$ (planar lamina only). Derivation: For a mass element at $(x,y)$: $I_x = \int y^2\,dm$, $I_y = \int x^2\,dm$, $I_z = \int(x^2+y^2)\,dm = I_x + I_y$.

Standard MIs: Thin rod ($L$): Centre, $\perp$: $\frac{1}{12}ML^2$. End, $\perp$: $\frac{1}{3}ML^2$. Thin ring ($R$): Centre, $\perp$ to plane: $MR^2$. Diameter: $\frac{1}{2}MR^2$. Disc ($R$): Centre, $\perp$ to plane: $\frac{1}{2}MR^2$. Diameter: $\frac{1}{4}MR^2$. Solid sphere ($R$): Any diameter: $\frac{2}{5}MR^2$. Hollow sphere ($R$): Any diameter: $\frac{2}{3}MR^2$.

**What does this mean for Applied Mechanics?** Memorise the standard MIs for rod, ring, disc, and sphere. For new axes, try parallel axes first (shift from CM). For planar bodies, use perpendicular axes to relate $I_x$, $I_y$, $I_z$. Always check: is the axis through the CM? If not, use parallel axes to get there first. Dimensions check: $I$ has dimensions $ML^2$.

**Setup:** Thin rod, length $L$, mass $M$. Find MI about an axis through one end, perpendicular to the rod.

**Solution:** $I_{CM} = \frac{1}{12}ML^2$, $d = L/2$. $I_{\text{end}} = I_{CM} + Md^2 = \frac{1}{12}ML^2 + M(L/2)^2 = \frac{1}{12}ML^2 + \frac{1}{4}ML^2 = \frac{1}{3}ML^2$.

**Key insight:** The parallel axes theorem always increases the MI — mass is farther from the new axis.

**Setup:** Rectangular lamina $a \times b$, mass $M$. Find MI about an axis through one corner, perpendicular to the plane.

**Solution:** $I_{\text{centre}} = \frac{1}{12}M(a^2+b^2)$ (about centre, perpendicular to plane). Distance from centre to corner: $d^2 = (a/2)^2 + (b/2)^2 = (a^2+b^2)/4$. $I_{\text{corner}} = I_{\text{centre}} + Md^2 = \frac{1}{12}M(a^2+b^2) + \frac{1}{4}M(a^2+b^2) = \frac{1}{3}M(a^2+b^2)$.

**Key insight:** The corner MI is exactly 4 times the centre MI for a rectangular lamina.
