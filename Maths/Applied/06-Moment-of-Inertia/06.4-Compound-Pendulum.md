A compound (physical) pendulum is any rigid body oscillating about a horizontal pivot that does not pass through its centre of mass. For small oscillations, the equation of motion is $\ddot{\theta} + (Mgh/I_O)\theta = 0$, which is SHM with $\omega = \sqrt{Mgh/I_O}$ and period $T = 2\pi\sqrt{I_O/(Mgh)}$. Using the parallel axes theorem, $I_O = I_{CM} + Mh^2 = M(k^2 + h^2)$, where $k$ is the radius of gyration about the CM and $h$ is the pivot-to-CM distance. The period becomes $T = 2\pi\sqrt{(k^2 + h^2)/(gh)}$. The equivalent simple pendulum length is $L_{\text{eq}} = (k^2 + h^2)/h = k^2/h + h$.

**The Intuition:** A swinging ruler — pivot it at one end and it oscillates. The period depends on how the mass is distributed (captured by $I_O$) and how far the CM is from the pivot (captured by $h$). The restoring torque is $\tau = -Mgh\sin\theta$, provided by gravity acting at the CM. The resistance to rotation is $I_O$. The ratio $\tau/I_O$ gives the angular acceleration, and hence the frequency. A larger $I_O$ means more resistance to rotation (slower oscillation). A larger $h$ means a stronger restoring torque (faster oscillation). The period balances these two effects.

**The Math:** Torque about pivot: $\tau = -Mgh\sin\theta \approx -Mgh\theta$. Newton's 2nd law: $I_O\ddot{\theta} = \tau$. So $\ddot{\theta} + (Mgh/I_O)\theta = 0$. Hence $\omega = \sqrt{Mgh/I_O}$.

Period: $T = 2\pi\sqrt{I_O/(Mgh)} = 2\pi\sqrt{(k^2 + h^2)/(gh)}$. Equivalent length: $L_{\text{eq}} = k^2/h + h$.

Conjugate points: If the pendulum is pivoted at distance $h$ from CM, the period is the same as when pivoted at distance $k^2/h$ from CM on the opposite side. This is the basis of Kater's pendulum.

Minimum period: $T(h) = 2\pi\sqrt{(k^2/h + h)/g}$. Setting $dT/dh = 0$ gives $h = k$, with $T_{\min} = 2\pi\sqrt{2k/g}$.

Simple pendulum is a special case: For a point mass at distance $L$ from pivot: $I_O = ML^2$, $h = L$, $k = 0$. Then $T = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$.

**What does this mean for Applied Mechanics?** Always identify $h$ (distance from pivot to CM) — this is the most common error. Use parallel axes: $I_O = I_{CM} + Mh^2 = M(k^2 + h^2)$. For conjugate points, the two pivot positions $h$ and $k^2/h$ give the same period. The minimum period occurs at $h = k$ — memorise $T_{\min} = 2\pi\sqrt{2k/g}$. For measuring $g$, use two pivot positions that give the same period (Kater's method).

**Setup:** Uniform rod, length $L = 1$ m, mass $M = 0.5$ kg, pivoted at one end. Find the period.

**Solution:** $h = L/2 = 0.5$ m (CM at midpoint). $I_O = \frac{1}{3}ML^2 = \frac{1}{3}(0.5)(1)^2 = 1/6$ kg$\cdot$m². $T = 2\pi\sqrt{I_O/(Mgh)} = 2\pi\sqrt{(1/6)/(0.5 \times 9.8 \times 0.5)} = 2\pi\sqrt{(1/6)/2.45} \approx 1.64$ s.

**Key insight:** The equivalent simple pendulum length is $L_{\text{eq}} = I_O/(Mh) = (1/6)/(0.5 \times 0.5) = 2/3$ m.

**Setup:** Uniform disc, radius $R = 0.3$ m, mass $M = 1$ kg, pivoted at a point on its rim. Find the period.

**Solution:** $h = R = 0.3$ m. $I_{CM} = \frac{1}{2}MR^2$, $I_O = I_{CM} + MR^2 = \frac{3}{2}MR^2$. $T = 2\pi\sqrt{I_O/(Mgh)} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{3}{2}R^2/(gR)} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{3R}{2g}} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{3 \times 0.3}{2 \times 9.8}} \approx 1.35$ s.

**Key insight:** The equivalent length is $L_{\text{eq}} = \frac{3}{2}R = 1.5R$.
