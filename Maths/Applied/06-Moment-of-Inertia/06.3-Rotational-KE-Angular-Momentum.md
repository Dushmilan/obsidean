A rigid body rotating with angular velocity $\omega$ about a fixed axis has rotational kinetic energy $K_{\text{rot}} = \frac{1}{2}I\omega^2$, where $I$ is the moment of inertia about the axis. For a body in general motion (translation + rotation), the total KE is $K_{\text{total}} = \frac{1}{2}Mv_{CM}^2 + \frac{1}{2}I_{CM}\omega^2$ — the sum of translational KE of the centre of mass and rotational KE about the CM. Angular momentum $L = I\omega$ is the rotational analogue of linear momentum. The angular momentum principle states $\tau_{\text{ext}} = dL/dt$: the rate of change of angular momentum equals the net external torque. When $\tau_{\text{ext}} = 0$, angular momentum is conserved ($L = I\omega = \text{constant}$).

**The Intuition:** A spinning ice skater — pulling arms in reduces $I$, so $\omega$ increases to conserve $L$. The rotational KE increases (work is done by the skater's muscles). Every mass element $dm$ at distance $r$ from the axis has speed $v = \omega r$ and KE $\frac{1}{2}dm\,\omega^2 r^2$. Summing gives $\frac{1}{2}\omega^2\int r^2\,dm = \frac{1}{2}I\omega^2$. The $r^2$ weighting in $I$ naturally captures how mass distribution affects rotational energy — mass far from the axis contributes more to both $I$ and $K_{\text{rot}}$.

**The Math:** Rotational KE: $K_{\text{rot}} = \frac{1}{2}I\omega^2$. Total KE (general motion): $K = \frac{1}{2}Mv_{CM}^2 + \frac{1}{2}I_{CM}\omega^2$. Angular momentum: $L = I\omega$. Torque: $\tau = I\alpha = dL/dt$. Work by torque: $W = \int \tau\,d\theta$. Power: $P = \tau\omega$.

Rolling constraint: $v = \omega r$, $a = \alpha r$. Rolling KE: $K = \frac{1}{2}Mv^2(1 + k^2/r^2)$. Rolling acceleration (incline): $a = \frac{g\sin\theta}{1 + k^2/r^2}$.

The separation of KE into CM and rotational parts is exact for any rigid body motion. Friction does no work in pure rolling — the contact point is instantaneously at rest, so the friction force acts through zero displacement. This is why energy conservation works for rolling problems. Angular momentum conservation is powerful: when no external torque acts, $I\omega = \text{constant}$. Changing $I$ (e.g., pulling arms in) changes $\omega$ proportionally.

**What does this mean for Applied Mechanics?** For general rigid body motion, always separate $K_{\text{total}}$ into CM and rotational parts. For rolling, use $v = \omega r$ to eliminate one variable. For conservation of angular momentum, check that $\tau_{\text{ext}} = 0$. Remember: friction in pure rolling does no work — use energy conservation. For acceleration down an incline, use $a = g\sin\theta/(1 + k^2/r^2)$.

**Setup:** Solid sphere ($k^2/r^2 = 2/5$) rolls down an incline of angle $\theta$ from rest. Find the acceleration and speed after distance $s$.

**Solution:** $a = \frac{g\sin\theta}{1 + k^2/r^2} = \frac{g\sin\theta}{1 + 2/5} = \frac{5}{7}g\sin\theta$. $v^2 = 2as = \frac{10}{7}gs\sin\theta \implies v = \sqrt{\frac{10gs\sin\theta}{7}}$.

**Key insight:** The acceleration is less than $g\sin\theta$ because some energy goes into rotational KE.

**Setup:** A disc ($I = \frac{1}{2}MR^2$) is spinning at $\omega_0$. A second disc ($I = \frac{1}{2}MR^2$) is dropped on top. Find the final angular velocity.

**Solution:** $L_{\text{initial}} = I\omega_0 = \frac{1}{2}MR^2\omega_0$. $L_{\text{final}} = (I + I)\omega_f = MR^2\omega_f$. Conservation: $\frac{1}{2}MR^2\omega_0 = MR^2\omega_f \implies \omega_f = \omega_0/2$.

**Key insight:** Doubling the moment of inertia halves the angular velocity (energy is lost to friction between the discs).
