# Proofs: Centre of Mass

**Syllabus:** GCE A/L Combined Mathematics (021) — Paper II, Topic 3.3

---

## Proof 1: Centre of Mass of a System of Particles

**Statement:** For particles of masses $m_i$ at positions $\vec{r}_i$, the centre of mass is
$$
\vec{r}_{\text{cm}} = \frac{\sum m_i \vec{r}_i}{\sum m_i}
$$

**Proof:**
Definition: CM is the point where the total mass can be concentrated with the same external effect.
Moment of system about origin: $\sum m_i \vec{r}_i$.
If mass $M = \sum m_i$ is concentrated at $\vec{r}_{\text{cm}}$, its moment is $M \vec{r}_{\text{cm}}$.
Equate: $M \vec{r}_{\text{cm}} = \sum m_i \vec{r}_i$.
$\Rightarrow \vec{r}_{\text{cm}} = \frac{\sum m_i \vec{r}_i}{M}$.
∎

## Proof 2: Centre of Mass of a Uniform Rod

**Statement:** CM of a uniform rod of length $L$ is at its midpoint.

**Proof:**
Place rod along x-axis from $0$ to $L$. Linear density $\lambda = M/L$.
$x_{\text{cm}} = \frac{1}{M}\int_0^L x \lambda\,dx = \frac{\lambda}{M}\left[\frac{x^2}{2}\right]_0^L = \frac{\lambda L^2}{2M} = \frac{M}{2M}L = \frac{L}{2}$.
∎

## Proof 3: Centre of Mass of a Uniform Triangular Lamina

**Statement:** CM of a uniform triangular lamina is at the centroid (intersection of medians), $\frac{1}{3}$ of the way from each side.

**Proof:**
Triangle vertices $(0,0), (a,0), (0,b)$. Equation of hypotenuse: $y = b(1 - x/a)$.
Area $A = \frac{1}{2}ab$. Mass per area $\sigma = M/A$.

$x_{\text{cm}} = \frac{1}{A}\iint x\,dA = \frac{1}{A}\int_0^a \int_0^{b(1-x/a)} x\,dy\,dx$
$= \frac{1}{A}\int_0^a x b(1-x/a)\,dx = \frac{b}{A}\left[\frac{x^2}{2} - \frac{x^3}{3a}\right]_0^a$
$= \frac{b}{A}\left(\frac{a^2}{2} - \frac{a^2}{3}\right) = \frac{b}{A}\cdot\frac{a^2}{6} = \frac{ba^2}{6(ab/2)} = \frac{a}{3}$.

By symmetry, $y_{\text{cm}} = \frac{b}{3}$.
∎

## Proof 4: Centre of Mass of a Uniform Semicircular Lamina

**Statement:** CM of a uniform semicircular lamina (radius $r$, diameter on x-axis) is at $(0, \frac{4r}{3\pi})$.

**Proof:**
Use polar coordinates: $y = r\sin\theta$, $dA = r\,dr\,d\theta$.
$x_{\text{cm}} = 0$ by symmetry.
$y_{\text{cm}} = \frac{1}{A}\iint y\,dA = \frac{1}{\frac{1}{2}\pi r^2}\int_0^\pi \int_0^r (r\sin\theta) r\,dr\,d\theta$
$= \frac{2}{\pi r^2}\int_0^\pi \sin\theta\,d\theta \int_0^r r^2\,dr = \frac{2}{\pi r^2} [-\cos\theta]_0^\pi \cdot \frac{r^3}{3}$
$= \frac{2}{\pi r^2} (2) \cdot \frac{r^3}{3} = \frac{4r}{3\pi}$.
∎

## Proof 5: Centre of Mass of a Uniform Solid Hemisphere

**Statement:** CM of a uniform solid hemisphere (radius $r$, base on xy-plane) is at $(0, 0, \frac{3r}{8})$.

**Proof:**
Use spherical coordinates or slice into disks.
Disk at height $z$: radius $\sqrt{r^2 - z^2}$, area $\pi(r^2 - z^2)$, mass $dm = \rho \pi(r^2 - z^2)dz$.
$z_{\text{cm}} = \frac{1}{M}\int_0^r z\,dm = \frac{\rho\pi}{M}\int_0^r z(r^2 - z^2)\,dz$
$= \frac{\rho\pi}{M}\left[\frac{r^2z^2}{2} - \frac{z^4}{4}\right]_0^r = \frac{\rho\pi}{M}\left(\frac{r^4}{2} - \frac{r^4}{4}\right) = \frac{\rho\pi r^4}{4M}$.
Mass $M = \rho \cdot \frac{2}{3}\pi r^3$.
$z_{\text{cm}} = \frac{\rho\pi r^4}{4} \cdot \frac{3}{2\rho\pi r^3} = \frac{3r}{8}$.
∎

## Proof 6: Centre of Mass of a Uniform Solid Cone

**Statement:** CM of a uniform solid cone (height $h$, base radius $R$) is at distance $\frac{h}{4}$ from the base on the axis.

**Proof:**
Slice into disks parallel to base at distance $z$ from vertex.
Radius at $z$: $r(z) = \frac{R}{h}z$.
Disk mass: $dm = \rho \pi r(z)^2 dz = \rho \pi \frac{R^2}{h^2}z^2 dz$.
$z_{\text{cm}} = \frac{1}{M}\int_0^h z\,dm = \frac{\rho\pi R^2}{M h^2}\int_0^h z^3 dz = \frac{\rho\pi R^2}{M h^2}\cdot\frac{h^4}{4} = \frac{\rho\pi R^2 h^2}{4M}$.
Mass $M = \frac{1}{3}\pi R^2 h \rho$.
$z_{\text{cm}} = \frac{\rho\pi R^2 h^2}{4} \cdot \frac{3}{\rho\pi R^2 h} = \frac{3h}{4}$ from vertex.
From base: $h - \frac{3h}{4} = \frac{h}{4}$.
∎

## Proof 7: Centre of Mass of a Uniform Hollow Hemisphere

**Statement:** CM of a uniform hollow hemisphere (radius $r$) is at distance $\frac{r}{2}$ from the base.

**Proof:**
Surface element: $dS = r^2\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi$, $z = r\cos\theta$.
Mass $dm = \sigma r^2\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi$.
$z_{\text{cm}} = \frac{1}{M}\int z\,dm = \frac{1}{M}\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^{\pi/2} (r\cos\theta)\sigma r^2\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi$
$= \frac{2\pi\sigma r^3}{M}\int_0^{\pi/2} \cos\theta\sin\theta\,d\theta = \frac{2\pi\sigma r^3}{M}\cdot\frac{1}{2} = \frac{\pi\sigma r^3}{M}$.
Mass $M = \sigma \cdot 2\pi r^2$.
$z_{\text{cm}} = \frac{\pi\sigma r^3}{2\pi\sigma r^2} = \frac{r}{2}$.
∎

## Proof 8: Centre of Mass of a Uniform Wire Bent as a Semicircle

**Statement:** CM of a uniform wire bent into a semicircle (radius $r$) is at distance $\frac{2r}{\pi}$ from the diameter.

**Proof:**
Wire element: $ds = r\,d\theta$, $y = r\sin\theta$.
Mass per length $\lambda$, total mass $M = \lambda \pi r$.
$y_{\text{cm}} = \frac{1}{M}\int y\,dm = \frac{\lambda}{M}\int_0^\pi (r\sin\theta) r\,d\theta = \frac{\lambda r^2}{M}[-\cos\theta]_0^\pi = \frac{\lambda r^2}{M}(2)$.
$y_{\text{cm}} = \frac{2\lambda r^2}{\lambda \pi r} = \frac{2r}{\pi}$.
∎

## Proof 9: Subtraction Method for Composite Bodies

**Statement:** For a body with a hole/cutout, treat the hole as negative mass. If full body has CM $\vec{r}_F$ and mass $M_F$, hole has CM $\vec{r}_H$ and mass $M_H$, then composite CM is
$$
\vec{r}_{\text{cm}} = \frac{M_F\vec{r}_F - M_H\vec{r}_H}{M_F - M_H}
$$

**Proof:**
Total mass $M = M_F - M_H$.
Moment of composite = moment of full body minus moment of hole (since hole is removed).
$M\vec{r}_{\text{cm}} = M_F\vec{r}_F - M_H\vec{r}_H$.
$\Rightarrow \vec{r}_{\text{cm}} = \frac{M_F\vec{r}_F - M_H\vec{r}_H}{M_F - M_H}$.
∎

## Proof 10: Suspension Principle

**Statement:** A body suspended from a point $P$ hangs with its centre of mass vertically below $P$.

**Proof:**
For equilibrium, the net moment about $P$ must be zero.
Forces: weight $W$ acting at CM, reaction $R$ at $P$.
Moment of $R$ about $P$ is zero (line of action passes through $P$).
Moment of $W$ about $P$: $\vec{r} \times (-W\hat{j})$ where $\vec{r} = \overrightarrow{P \to \text{CM}}$.
This is zero only if $\vec{r}$ is parallel to $\hat{j}$ (vertical).
Thus CM lies vertically below $P$.
∎
