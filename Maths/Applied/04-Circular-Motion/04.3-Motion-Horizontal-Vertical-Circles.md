This topic splits into two distinct regimes. Horizontal circles (on a table, inside a cylinder) involve constant speed and straightforward centripetal force analysis. Vertical circles are richer: gravity speeds up the particle on the way down and slows it on the way up, so the speed varies with position. Energy conservation relates speeds at different heights, while Newton's 2nd law in the radial direction gives the tension or normal reaction at each point. The critical question for vertical circles is: what is the minimum speed at the bottom (or top) for the particle to complete a full circle?

**The Intuition:** Swinging a bucket of water in a vertical circle — at the top, the water stays in if you spin fast enough because gravity alone provides more than the required centripetal force. At the bottom, both tension and gravity act radially (tension inward, gravity outward), so tension must be large. At the top, both tension and gravity act inward, so tension can be small or zero. Energy conservation dictates that the particle is fastest at the bottom (lowest PE) and slowest at the top (highest PE). The tension must always be non-negative (for a string), which sets the minimum speed condition.

**The Math:** Combine energy conservation $\frac{1}{2}mv^2 = \frac{1}{2}mu^2 - mgr(1-\cos\theta)$ with radial Newton's 2nd law $T - mg\cos\theta = mv^2/r$ to get:

Tension at angle $\theta$: $T = mu^2/r - 2mg + 3mg\cos\theta$. Speed at angle $\theta$: $v^2 = u^2 - 2gr(1-\cos\theta)$.

String vs rod — fundamentally different constraints: A string can only pull ($T \geq 0$); a rod can push or pull. Minimum speed at the top: $\sqrt{gr}$ for a string, $0$ for a rod.

Minimum speed at bottom: String: $u_{\min} = \sqrt{5gr}$. Rod: $u_{\min} = 2\sqrt{gr}$.

When $T = 0$ for a string, the particle leaves the circular path and becomes a projectile. For $u < \sqrt{5gr}$, the string goes slack before the top.

Particle on outside of sphere: Regardless of $R$ or $g$, a particle starting from rest at the top of a smooth sphere loses contact at $\cos\theta = 2/3$ ($\theta \approx 48.2°$). This is a universal result that comes from setting $R_N = 0$ in the radial equation and using energy conservation.

**What does this mean for Applied Mechanics?** For vertical circles, always use energy conservation to relate speeds at different positions. Draw a diagram with $\theta$ measured from the bottom (or top) and label the radial direction clearly. For string problems, check $T \geq 0$ at the most critical point (usually the top). For rod problems, the critical condition is $v_{\text{top}} \geq 0$. For particle-on-sphere, set the normal reaction to zero and solve for $\theta$.

**Setup:** Particle on string of length $r$, given speed $u = \sqrt{3gr}$ at bottom. At what angle does the string go slack? What is the maximum height?

**Solution:** $v^2 = u^2 - 2gr(1-\cos\theta) = 3gr - 2gr + 2gr\cos\theta = gr + 2gr\cos\theta$. String slack when $T = 0$: $0 = mv^2/r + mg\cos\theta = m(g + 2g\cos\theta) + mg\cos\theta$. $3mg\cos\theta = -mg \implies \cos\theta = -1/3 \implies \theta \approx 109.5°$. Height above bottom: $h = r(1-\cos\theta) = r(1 + 1/3) = 4r/3$.

**Key insight:** The particle never reaches the top — it becomes a projectile before getting there.

**Setup:** Particle placed at rest on top of a smooth sphere of radius $R$. At what angle does it lose contact? What is its speed at that point?

**Solution:** $R_N = mg(3\cos\theta - 2) = 0 \implies \cos\theta = 2/3 \implies \theta \approx 48.2°$. $v^2 = 2gR(1 - 2/3) = 2gR/3 \implies v = \sqrt{2gR/3}$.

**Key insight:** The result is independent of $R$ and $g$ — a universal geometric result.
