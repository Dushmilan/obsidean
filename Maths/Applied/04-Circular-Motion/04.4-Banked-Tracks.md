A vehicle of mass $m$ moves in a horizontal circle of radius $r$ on a banked track inclined at angle $\theta$ to the horizontal. Three forces act: weight $mg$ (down), normal reaction $R$ (perpendicular to the surface), and friction $F$ (parallel to the surface, direction depending on speed). At the ideal "design speed" $v_0$, no friction is needed: the horizontal component of the normal reaction alone provides the centripetal force. This gives $\tan\theta = v^2/(rg)$, hence $v_0 = \sqrt{rg\tan\theta}$. At higher speeds, friction acts downward. At lower speeds, friction acts upward.

**The Intuition:** Tilting a tray and rolling a ball — at just the right speed, the ball circles without sliding up or down the tray. Too fast, it slides up; too slow, it slides down. On a flat road, friction alone provides centripetal force. Banking the road lets the normal reaction contribute a horizontal component, reducing the reliance on friction. At the design speed, friction is zero. The steeper the bank and the tighter the curve, the higher the design speed. This is why highway exit ramps are banked and race tracks have banked corners.

**The Math:** Design speed (no friction): $v_0 = \sqrt{rg\tan\theta}$. From $R\cos\theta = mg$, $R\sin\theta = mv^2/r$, dividing gives $\tan\theta = v^2/(rg)$.

With friction: $v_{\max} = \sqrt{\frac{rg(\tan\theta + \mu)}{1 - \mu\tan\theta}}$. $v_{\min} = \sqrt{\frac{rg(\tan\theta - \mu)}{1 + \mu\tan\theta}}$.

Friction direction depends on speed, not position: At $v > v_0$, friction acts down the bank (opposing upward sliding). At $v < v_0$, friction acts up the bank (opposing downward sliding). At $v = v_0$, friction is zero. If $\tan\theta \leq \mu$, the vehicle will not slip down even at rest ($v_{\min} = 0$).

Overturning vs skidding: depends on the ratio $d/h$ (half-track-width to centre-of-mass height). A high, narrow vehicle overturns first; a low, wide vehicle skids first.

**What does this mean for Applied Mechanics?** Always resolve forces vertically and horizontally — not parallel/perpendicular to the surface. For the design speed, set friction to zero. For max/min speed, add friction in the correct direction. Check whether $v_{\min}$ is real: if $\tan\theta \leq \mu$, then $v_{\min} = 0$. For overturning, compare $v_{\text{overturn}} = \sqrt{grd/h}$ with $v_{\max}$ from friction.

**Setup:** $r = 200$ m, $\theta = 15°$, $g = 9.8$ m/s². Find the design speed (no friction needed).

**Solution:** $v_0 = \sqrt{rg\tan\theta} = \sqrt{200 \times 9.8 \times \tan 15°} = \sqrt{1960 \times 0.2679} \approx \sqrt{525} \approx 22.9$ m/s.

**Key insight:** At this speed, no friction is needed — the normal reaction alone provides the centripetal force.

**Setup:** $r = 100$ m, $\theta = 20°$, $\mu = 0.4$, $g = 9.8$ m/s². Find $v_0$, $v_{\max}$, and $v_{\min}$.

**Solution:** $v_0 = \sqrt{100 \times 9.8 \times \tan 20°} \approx 18.9$ m/s. $v_{\max} = \sqrt{\frac{100 \times 9.8(\tan 20° + 0.4)}{1 - 0.4\tan 20°}} = \sqrt{\frac{980 \times 0.764}{0.8544}} \approx \sqrt{876} \approx 29.6$ m/s. $v_{\min}$: imaginary since $\tan 20° \approx 0.364 < \mu = 0.4$, so $v_{\min} = 0$.

**Key insight:** Since $\tan\theta < \mu$, the vehicle will not slip down even at rest.
