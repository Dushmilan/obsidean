
## Definition

A particle moves in a circle at **constant speed**: the velocity vector changes direction continuously, so there is an acceleration even though the speed is fixed.

$$a_c = \frac{v^2}{r} = \omega^2 r \quad (\text{radially inward}), \qquad F_c = \frac{mv^2}{r} = m\omega^2 r$$

**Centripetal force is not a new kind of force** — it's the net radial component of the real forces (tension, normal, friction, gravity).

## The Intuition

A ball on a string: the string pulls inward, constantly deflecting the ball from its straight-line path; without it the ball flies off tangentially. Take velocity vectors at two nearby points — same magnitude, different direction; the difference $\Delta\vec{v}$ points toward the centre. Any curved motion needs acceleration perpendicular to velocity; for a circle, that's always radial-inward.

## The Toolkit

| Quantity | Formula |
|----------|---------|
| Centripetal acceleration | $a_c = \dfrac{v^2}{r} = \omega^2 r$ |
| Centripetal force | $F_c = \dfrac{mv^2}{r} = m\omega^2 r$ |
| Angular velocity | $\omega = \dfrac{v}{r}$ |
| Period | $T = \dfrac{2\pi r}{v} = \dfrac{2\pi}{\omega}$ |
| Frequency | $f = \dfrac{1}{T}$ |
| Arc length | $s = r\theta$ |

## Derivation

Geometric: two velocity vectors at angle $\Delta\theta$; the chord between their tips is $\Delta v = v\Delta\theta$, so $a = v\frac{d\theta}{dt} = v\omega = v^2/r$. Calculus: differentiate the position $\vec{r} = r(\cos\omega t, \sin\omega t)$ twice. [Full derivations: 04.1-Proofs-Uniform-Circular-Motion]

## Method

1. **Draw the FBD** — identify which real force(s) provide the inward radial resultant.
2. Write the radial equation: $\sum F_{\text{radial}} = mv^2/r$.
3. Convert between $v, \omega, T$ as needed.
4. Speed is constant — the tangential acceleration is zero; all acceleration is radial.

## Worked Examples

**Setup:** A 2 kg ball on a 1 m string moves at 3 m/s. Find the tension.

**Solution:** $T = \frac{mv^2}{r} = \frac{2\cdot9}{1} = 18$ N (the string is the only radial force).

**Key insight:** The centripetal force is just the tension here — plug in the numbers.

---

**Setup:** A car rounds a flat curve of radius 50 m at 15 m/s, $\mu = 0.5$. Does it slip?

**Solution:** Required $a = \frac{v^2}{r} = 4.5$ m/s². Available $a = \mu g = 5$ m/s². $4.5 < 5$ → no slip.

**Key insight:** Friction provides the centripetal force; compare required vs available.

## Common Traps

- Calling the centripetal force a "fifth force" — it's the *resultant* of real forces
- Confusing $\omega^2 r$ with $\omega r$ (that's $v$)
- Using diameter instead of radius
- Forgetting the acceleration is *toward the centre*, not along the motion
- Speed constant ≠ zero acceleration (direction changes)
