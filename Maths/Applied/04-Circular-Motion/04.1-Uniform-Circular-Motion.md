When a particle moves in a circle at constant speed, its velocity vector is continuously changing direction even though its magnitude stays fixed. This change in direction constitutes an acceleration, which must be caused by a real inward force. The centripetal acceleration formula $a_c = v^2/r$ is derived either geometrically (by considering the change in velocity vectors over a small angle) or via calculus (by differentiating the position vector twice). The centripetal force is not a new kind of force — it is simply the net radial component of whatever real forces act on the particle: tension in a string, normal reaction, friction, or gravity.

**The Intuition:** A ball on a string swinging in a circle — the string pulls inward, constantly deflecting the ball from a straight-line path. Without the string, the ball would fly off tangentially. Imagine the velocity vectors at two nearby points on the circle. They have the same magnitude but different directions. The difference vector $\Delta\vec{v}$ points toward the centre. Shrinking the time interval gives the instantaneous acceleration, which is purely radial. Any curved motion requires acceleration perpendicular to the velocity. For a circle, that perpendicular direction is always toward the centre.

**The Math:** Centripetal acceleration: $a_c = v^2/r = \omega^2 r$, directed radially inward. Centripetal force: $F_c = mv^2/r = m\omega^2 r$ (net radial force).

Relations: Angular velocity: $\omega = v/r$. Period: $T = 2\pi/\omega = 2\pi r/v$. Frequency: $f = 1/T = \omega/2\pi$. Tangential speed: $v = r\omega$.

Centripetal force is not a "new" type of force — it is the net radial component of real forces. Always identify the physical force providing the centripetal effect (tension, friction, gravity, normal reaction). Centrifugal force is a fictitious force that appears in a rotating (non-inertial) frame. Non-uniform circular motion adds a tangential component: $a_t = r\alpha$ (tangential, changing speed) and $a_c = v^2/r$ (radial, changing direction). Total: $a = \sqrt{a_t^2 + a_c^2}$.

**What does this mean for Applied Mechanics?** Always draw a free-body diagram and resolve forces radially toward the centre. Identify which real force provides the centripetal component before writing equations. For vertical circles, use energy conservation to relate speeds at different heights. Remember that centripetal force is a resultant, not a separate force you add to a diagram.

**Setup:** A 0.5 kg mass on a 1.2 m string rotates horizontally on a frictionless table. The string breaks at 40 N. Find the maximum angular velocity $\omega$ and maximum speed $v$.

**Solution:** $T = m\omega^2 r \leq 40 \implies 0.5 \cdot \omega^2 \cdot 1.2 \leq 40 \implies \omega^2 \leq \frac{40}{0.6} = \frac{200}{3}$. $\omega_{\max} = \sqrt{200/3} \approx 8.16$ rad/s. $v_{\max} = r\omega_{\max} \approx 9.80$ m/s.

**Key insight:** The breaking tension sets the maximum centripetal force, which limits the speed.

**Setup:** A satellite orbits Earth (radius $R_E = 6370$ km) at height $h = 400$ km. Find the orbital speed and period.

**Solution:** Orbital radius $r = R_E + h = 6770$ km $= 6.77 \times 10^6$ m. At surface: $g = GM/R_E^2 \implies GM = gR_E^2$. At orbit: $GMm/r^2 = mv^2/r \implies v = \sqrt{GM/r} = R_E\sqrt{g/r}$. $v = 6.37 \times 10^6 \sqrt{9.8/(6.77 \times 10^6)} \approx 7670$ m/s. $T = 2\pi r/v \approx 5540$ s $\approx 92$ min.

**Key insight:** The same centripetal force formula applies to orbital mechanics — gravity provides the centripetal force.
