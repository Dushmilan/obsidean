This topic extends vertical circle ideas to spherical geometry. A particle of mass $m$ moves on a smooth (frictionless) spherical surface of radius $R$. Two main cases arise: a particle on the outside of the sphere (sliding down from the top), and a particle on the inside (like a bead on a wire hoop or a particle in a spherical bowl). For the outside case, the particle starts at the top and slides under gravity. The normal reaction decreases as the particle descends, eventually reaching zero at a specific angle. Setting $R_N = 0$ and using energy conservation yields the universal result $\cos\theta = 2/3$ — independent of $m$, $R$, and $g$.

**The Intuition:** Sliding down a globe — at first, the surface pushes you outward (normal reaction). As you slide further down, you move faster and the required centripetal force increases, but the component of gravity providing it decreases. Eventually, the surface can no longer push hard enough, and you fly off. On the outside, gravity has two jobs: pulling you down (tangential, increasing speed) and pulling you inward (radial, providing centripetal force). As $\theta$ increases, the radial component of gravity decreases while the required centripetal force increases. The normal reaction makes up the difference until it hits zero. The result $\cos\theta = 2/3$ is a geometric fixed point.

**The Math:** Outside sphere: radial equation $mg\cos\theta - R_N = mv^2/R$; energy $v^2 = 2gR(1-\cos\theta)$; set $R_N = 0$ to get $\cos\theta = 2/3$.

Inside sphere: radial equation $R_N - mg\cos\theta = mv^2/R$; set $R_N \geq 0$ at top for complete circles: $u \geq \sqrt{5gR}$.

Normal reaction (outside, from rest): $R_N = mg(3\cos\theta - 2)$. Contact lost (outside, from rest): $\cos\theta = 2/3$, $\theta \approx 48.2°$. Speed at contact loss: $v = \sqrt{2gR/3}$.

Inside sphere is equivalent to string vertical circle: the normal reaction $R_N$ plays the same role as tension $T$ — both must be $\geq 0$. The condition for complete circles is $u \geq \sqrt{5gR}$, identical to the string case.

Conical motion on a sphere: A particle can also move in a horizontal circle at constant height on a smooth sphere. The angular velocity is $\omega = \sqrt{g/(R\cos\alpha)}$, identical in form to the conical pendulum with $L \to R$.

If given initial speed $u > 0$ at top, contact is lost earlier: $\cos\theta = 2/3 + u^2/(3gR)$. If $u^2 \geq gR$, the particle leaves immediately at the top.

**What does this mean for Applied Mechanics?** For outside sphere: set $R_N = 0$ in the radial equation and use energy conservation to find $\theta$. For inside sphere: check $R_N \geq 0$ at the top (most critical point) for complete circles. Remember the direction of $R_N$: outward for outside sphere, inward for inside sphere. After the particle leaves the sphere, treat it as a standard projectile problem.

**Setup:** $R = 0.5$ m, $g = 9.8$ m/s². Particle released from rest at top. At what angle does it lose contact?

**Solution:** $R_N = mg(3\cos\theta - 2) = 0 \implies \cos\theta = 2/3 \implies \theta \approx 48.2°$.

**Key insight:** This is a universal geometric result — independent of $R$ and $g$.

**Setup:** Particle leaves outside sphere at $\theta = \cos^{-1}(2/3)$ with $v = \sqrt{2gR/3}$. Find horizontal distance from centre where it hits the ground (ground at sphere centre level).

**Solution:** Initial height: $y_0 = R\cos\theta = 2R/3$. Initial velocity: horizontal $v_x = v = \sqrt{2gR/3}$, vertical $v_y = 0$. Time to fall: $y_0 = \frac{1}{2}gt^2 \implies t = \sqrt{4R/(3g)}$. Horizontal distance: $x = v_x t = \sqrt{2gR/3} \cdot \sqrt{4R/(3g)} = \sqrt{8R^2/9} = \frac{2\sqrt{2}}{3}R$.

**Key insight:** The subsequent projectile motion can be analyzed using standard kinematics.
