# The Dot Product

The dot product is the first of two ways to "multiply" vectors. Unlike scalar multiplication, which produces a vector, the dot product yields a scalar — it measures how aligned two vectors are.

**The Intuition:** Project $\mathbf{v}$ onto $\mathbf{u}$. The scalar projection tells you how much of $\mathbf{v}$ lies along $\mathbf{u}$. Multiply by $\|\mathbf{u}\|$ to get the dot product. If they point the same way, the dot product is positive and large. If they are perpendicular, it is zero — one vector has no component in the other's direction.

**The Math:** For $\mathbf{u} = \langle u_1, u_2, u_3 \rangle$ and $\mathbf{v} = \langle v_1, v_2, v_3 \rangle$:

- **Algebraic:** $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = u_1 v_1 + u_2 v_2 + u_3 v_3$
- **Geometric:** $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = \|\mathbf{u}\|\;\|\mathbf{v}\|\cos\theta$

The geometric formula follows from the law of cosines applied to the triangle formed by $\mathbf{u}$, $\mathbf{v}$, and $\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{v}$. Key properties: commutative, distributive, $\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{a} = \|\mathbf{a}\|^2$, and $\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = 0$ iff $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ are orthogonal.

From this we get the angle between vectors: $\cos\theta = \dfrac{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{u}\|\;\|\mathbf{v}\|}$. The scalar projection of $\mathbf{v}$ onto $\mathbf{u}$ is $\text{comp}_{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{v}) = \dfrac{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{u}\|}$, and the vector projection is $\text{proj}_{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{v}) = \dfrac{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{u}\|^2}\,\mathbf{u}$. Work done by a constant force is $W = \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{D}$.

**Setup:** Find the angle between $\mathbf{u} = \langle 1, 1, 0 \rangle$ and $\mathbf{v} = \langle 1, 0, 1 \rangle$.

**Solution:** $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = 1$, $\|\mathbf{u}\| = \sqrt{2}$, $\|\mathbf{v}\| = \sqrt{2}$. So $\cos\theta = 1/2$ and $\theta = 60°$.

**Key insight:** Compute the dot product and both magnitudes, then solve for $\theta$ using $\arccos$.

**Setup:** Let $\mathbf{u} = \langle 2, 1, -1 \rangle$ and $\mathbf{v} = \langle 1, 3, 2 \rangle$. Find the scalar and vector projection of $\mathbf{v}$ onto $\mathbf{u}$.

**Solution:** $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = 3$, $\|\mathbf{u}\|^2 = 6$. Scalar projection: $3/\sqrt{6} = \sqrt{6}/2$. Vector projection: $(3/6)\langle 2, 1, -1 \rangle = \langle 1, 1/2, -1/2 \rangle$.

**Key insight:** The scalar projection is the signed length of $\mathbf{v}$'s shadow on $\mathbf{u}$. The vector projection scales $\mathbf{u}$ to that length.

**Setup:** A force $\mathbf{F} = \langle 3, 0, -2 \rangle$ N moves an object from $A(0,1,0)$ to $B(4,1,3)$ m. Find the work done.

**Solution:** $\mathbf{D} = \overrightarrow{AB} = \langle 4, 0, 3 \rangle$. $W = \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{D} = 12 + 0 - 6 = 6$ J.

**Key insight:** Work is the dot product of force and displacement. Only the component of force in the direction of displacement does work.

### A/L Additional Notes

The dot product bridges algebra and geometry. Given two vectors in component form, you can instantly find the angle between them or determine whether they are perpendicular. Projection is the hidden workhorse — decomposing a vector into components parallel and perpendicular to another is fundamental to physics and statistics.

The perpendicular component of $\mathbf{a}$ relative to $\mathbf{b}$ is: $\mathbf{a}_\perp = \mathbf{a} - \text{proj}_{\mathbf{b}}\mathbf{a}$

### A/L Worked Example: Finding λ for Perpendicularity

**Setup:** Find $\lambda$ such that $\mathbf{a} = \lambda\mathbf{i} + 2\mathbf{j} + 3\mathbf{k}$ is perpendicular to $\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{i} - 4\mathbf{j} + 2\mathbf{k}$.

**Solution:** $\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = \lambda - 8 + 6 = 0 \Rightarrow \lambda = 2$.

**Key insight:** Perpendicularity gives a linear equation in the unknown.
