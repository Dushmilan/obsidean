Polar coordinates locate a point by its distance from the origin ($r$) and its angle from the positive x-axis ($\theta$), instead of by horizontal and vertical distances. Many curves -- spirals, cardioids, conics with focus at origin -- have much simpler equations in polar form. Conic sections have a beautifully unified polar form: $r=\frac{l}{1+e\cos\theta}$.

**The Intuition:** Imagine giving directions by saying "walk 5 metres, then turn 60 degrees and walk 3 metres." The first number is $r$, the second is $\theta$. A radar screen works the same way: each blip is located by how far from the centre ($r$) and what angle ($\theta$). For objects moving in circles or spirals, distance and angle are the natural quantities.

**The Math:** Conversion: $x = r\cos\theta,\; y = r\sin\theta$ and $r = \sqrt{x^2+y^2},\; \theta = \tan^{-1}(y/x)$

Quadrant adjustment: $\tan^{-1}(y/x)$ only gives correct angle in Quadrant I. Add $\pi$ for QII and QIII.

Distance: $\sqrt{r_1^2+r_2^2-2r_1r_2\cos(\theta_1-\theta_2)}$ (cosine rule in disguise)

Area: $\frac{1}{2}\int_{\alpha}^{\beta} r^2\,d\theta$

Arc length: $\int_{\alpha}^{\beta} \sqrt{r^2+(dr/d\theta)^2}\,d\theta$

Conic (focus at pole): $r = \frac{l}{1+e\cos\theta}$ where $l$ = semi-latus rectum, $e$ = eccentricity

Tangent gradient: $\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{r\cos\theta+r'\sin\theta}{-r\sin\theta+r'\cos\theta}$

**What does this mean for Pure Mathematics?** The unified conic form $r=\frac{l}{1+e\cos\theta}$ gives the parabola ($e=1$), ellipse ($0<e<1$), and hyperbola ($e>1$) in one equation. The area element $\frac{1}{2}r^2 d\theta$ comes from the triangle formula and is essential for polar integration.

### Worked Examples

**Setup:** Convert $(-2, 2)$ to polar.

**Solution:**
1. $r = \sqrt{4+4} = 2\sqrt{2}$
2. $\theta = \tan^{-1}(2/(-2)) = \tan^{-1}(-1)$. Quadrant II, so $\theta = \pi - \pi/4 = 3\pi/4$

**Key insight:** The calculator gives $-\pi/4$, but the point is in Quadrant II, so add $\pi$.

---

**Setup:** Area of circle $r = 2a\cos\theta$.

**Solution:**
1. Limits: $-\pi/2$ to $\pi/2$
2. Area $= \frac{1}{2}\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2} 4a^2\cos^2\theta\,d\theta = \pi a^2$

**Key insight:** This confirms the standard circle area formula, derived using polar integration.

---

**Setup:** Distance between $(2, \pi/3)$ and $(3, \pi/6)$.

**Solution:**
1. $d = \sqrt{4+9-2(2)(3)\cos(\pi/6)} = \sqrt{13-6\sqrt{3}}$

**Key insight:** The polar distance formula is the cosine rule in disguise.
