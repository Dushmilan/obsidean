
## Definition

Polar coordinates locate a point by its **distance from the origin** $r$ and its **angle from the positive $x$-axis** $\theta$, instead of horizontal/vertical distances.

Conversion: $x = r\cos\theta,\ y = r\sin\theta$ and $r = \sqrt{x^2+y^2}$, $\theta = \tan^{-1}(y/x)$ (with quadrant adjustment).

Many curves — spirals, cardioids, conics with focus at the origin — have much simpler polar equations.

## The Intuition

Directions like "walk 5 metres, then 60° that way": first number $r$, second $\theta$. A radar screen works the same way — each blip is located by range $r$ and bearing $\theta$. For motion in circles or spirals, distance and angle are the natural quantities.

## The Toolkit

| Result | Formula |
|--------|---------|
| Polar → Cartesian | $x = r\cos\theta,\ y = r\sin\theta$ |
| Cartesian → Polar | $r = \sqrt{x^2+y^2},\ \theta = \tan^{-1}(y/x)$ |
| Distance between points | $\sqrt{r_1^2 + r_2^2 - 2r_1r_2\cos(\theta_1-\theta_2)}$ |
| Area | $\dfrac12 \int_\alpha^\beta r^2\,d\theta$ |
| Arc length | $\int_\alpha^\beta \sqrt{r^2 + (dr/d\theta)^2}\,d\theta$ |
| Conic (focus at pole) | $r = \dfrac{l}{1 + e\cos\theta}$ ($l$ semi-latus rectum, $e$ eccentricity) |
| Tangent gradient | $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{r\cos\theta + r'\sin\theta}{-r\sin\theta + r'\cos\theta}$ |

## Derivation

Conversion comes from the definitions of $\cos\theta, \sin\theta$ on the right triangle. Distance is the cosine rule: sides $r_1, r_2$ with included angle $|\theta_1 - \theta_2|$. Area: a sector of angle $d\theta$ and radius $r$ has area $\frac12 r^2 d\theta$ (triangle area formula) — integrate. The unified conic form collapses circle ($e=0$), parabola ($e=1$), ellipse ($0<e<1$), hyperbola ($e>1$) into one equation. [Full derivations: 02.7-Polar-Coordinates-Proof]

## Method

1. **Converting a point:** compute $r$, then find $\theta$ **by quadrant** — $\tan^{-1}$ alone is wrong in QII/QIII (add $\pi$).
2. **Polar area:** identify the curve's angular limits (where $r = 0$, or where the loop closes), integrate $\frac12 r^2$.
3. **Polar distance:** use the cosine-rule form directly — no Cartesian round-trip needed.
4. **Curve sketching:** note $r$ as a function of $\theta$ — positive/negative $r$, symmetry, periodicity.

## Worked Examples

**Setup:** Convert $(-2, 2)$ to polar.

**Solution:** $r = \sqrt{4+4} = 2\sqrt{2}$; $\theta = \tan^{-1}(2/(-2)) = \tan^{-1}(-1)$. QII, so $\theta = \pi - \pi/4 = 3\pi/4$.

**Key insight:** The calculator gives $-\pi/4$, but the point is in QII — add $\pi$.

---

**Setup:** Area of the circle $r = 2a\cos\theta$.

**Solution:** Limits $-\pi/2$ to $\pi/2$: Area $= \frac12\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2} 4a^2\cos^2\theta\,d\theta = \pi a^2$.

**Key insight:** Polar integration reproduces the standard circle area — a built-in check.

---

**Setup:** Distance between $(2, \pi/3)$ and $(3, \pi/6)$.

**Solution:** $d = \sqrt{4 + 9 - 2(2)(3)\cos(\pi/6)} = \sqrt{13 - 6\sqrt{3}}$.

**Key insight:** The polar distance formula is the cosine rule in disguise.

## Common Traps

- Using $\tan^{-1}$ without the quadrant adjustment — the classic sign error
- Negative $r$ — the point is on the opposite ray (angle + $\pi$)
- Area limits: finding them from $r = 0$ crossings, not guesswork
- Forgetting the $\frac12$ in polar area
- Conic form $\frac{l}{1+e\cos\theta}$ vs the $\sin\theta$ variant (rotated axis) — check which axis
