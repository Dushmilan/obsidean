
## Definition

A **Cartesian coordinate system** assigns each point in the plane an ordered pair $(x, y)$ relative to two perpendicular axes meeting at the **origin**. Every measurement — distance, midpoint, section, area — follows from one idea: locations are pairs of numbers, and geometry reduces to Pythagoras.

## The Intuition

Walking a city grid: to get from one corner to another you walk some blocks east and some north. The straight-line shortcut is the hypotenuse of the right triangle formed by those two walks. Moving $a$ units horizontally and $b$ vertically gives a direct path of $\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$ — not a new theorem, just Pythagoras applied to coordinate differences.

## The Toolkit

For $A(x_1, y_1)$, $B(x_2, y_2)$:

| Result | Formula |
|--------|---------|
| Distance | $AB = \sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2 + (y_2-y_1)^2}$ |
| Midpoint | $\left(\dfrac{x_1+x_2}{2},\ \dfrac{y_1+y_2}{2}\right)$ |
| Internal division $m:n$ | $\left(\dfrac{mx_2+nx_1}{m+n},\ \dfrac{my_2+ny_1}{m+n}\right)$ |
| External division $m:n$ | $\left(\dfrac{mx_2-nx_1}{m-n},\ \dfrac{my_2-ny_1}{m-n}\right)$ |
| Triangle area | $\tfrac12\big|x_1(y_2-y_3) + x_2(y_3-y_1) + x_3(y_1-y_2)\big|$ |
| Centroid | $\left(\dfrac{x_1+x_2+x_3}{3},\ \dfrac{y_1+y_2+y_3}{3}\right)$ |
| Polar → Cartesian | $x = r\cos\theta,\ y = r\sin\theta$ |
| Cartesian → Polar | $r = \sqrt{x^2+y^2},\ \theta = \tan^{-1}(y/x)$ |

## Derivation

Distance: the differences $\Delta x, \Delta y$ form the legs of a right triangle; the distance is the hypotenuse. Midpoint: averaging the coordinates gives the point halfway along the segment. Internal division: a weighted average with weights $m, n$ — closer to $B$ when $m > n$. Triangle area: the shoelace/determinant formula (area of the parallelogram spanned by two sides, halved). [Full derivations: 02.1-Cartesian-Coordinates-Distance-Proof]

## Method

1. **Distance/geometry problems:** assign coordinates, apply the distance or section formula directly.
2. **Collinearity:** show one point divides the other two in a fixed ratio, or that the area is zero.
3. **Triangle centre problems:** compute centroid by averaging vertices.
4. **Polar conversion:** find $r = \sqrt{x^2+y^2}$, then pick $\theta$ **by quadrant** — $\tan^{-1}(y/x)$ alone gives the wrong angle in QII/QIII (add $180^\circ$).

## Worked Examples

**Setup:** Points $A(3,-2)$ and $B(-1,4)$. Find distance and midpoint.

**Solution:** $AB = \sqrt{(-1-3)^2 + (4+2)^2} = \sqrt{16+36} = 2\sqrt{13}$; midpoint $\left(\frac{3+(-1)}{2}, \frac{-2+4}{2}\right) = (1,1)$.

**Key insight:** Subtract in the same order for both coordinates: $(x_2-x_1,\ y_2-y_1)$.

---

**Setup:** $A(1,2), B(4,6), C(5,2)$. Find the area.

**Solution:** $\frac12\big|1(6-2) + 4(2-2) + 5(2-6)\big| = \frac12|4 + 0 - 20| = \frac12 \cdot 16 = 8$.

**Key insight:** The absolute value turns $-16$ into $+16$; vertex ordering changes sign, not area.

---

**Setup:** Convert $(3,4)$ to polar.

**Solution:** $r = \sqrt{9+16} = 5$; $\theta = \tan^{-1}(4/3) \approx 53.13^\circ$ (Q I — no adjustment).

**Key insight:** Both coordinates positive → Quadrant I → no angle adjustment.

## Common Traps

- Mixing up the subtraction order in distance (signs square away, but be consistent)
- Forgetting the $\frac12$ in the area formula
- External division formula sign errors — the $-$ goes with $m,n$ in the numerator
- $\tan^{-1}$ giving the wrong quadrant for $\theta$ — always check the sign of $x, y$
- Interpreting the shoelace determinant sign — take the absolute value
