
## Definition

For a general triangle with sides $a, b, c$ opposite angles $A, B, C$:

- **Sine rule:** $\dfrac{a}{\sin A} = \dfrac{b}{\sin B} = \dfrac{c}{\sin C} = 2R$ ($R$ = circumradius)
- **Cosine rule:** $a^2 = b^2 + c^2 - 2bc\cos A$, or $\cos A = \dfrac{b^2+c^2-a^2}{2bc}$
- **Area:** $\Delta = \tfrac12 bc\sin A = \sqrt{s(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)} = \dfrac{abc}{4R} = rs$

Right-angled trigonometry only works with a right angle; these rules handle every triangle.

## The Intuition

The sine rule: in any triangle, the ratio of a side to the sine of its opposite angle is constant — "longer sides face bigger angles, and the ratio is exactly $2R$." Every triangle fits in a circumcircle; the sine rule connects each side-angle pair to that circle's diameter. The cosine rule generalises Pythagoras: the $-2bc\cos A$ term measures how far from a right angle the triangle is.

## The Toolkit

| Result | Formula | Use when |
|--------|---------|----------|
| Sine rule | $\dfrac{a}{\sin A} = \dfrac{b}{\sin B} = \dfrac{c}{\sin C} = 2R$ | AAS, ASA, SSA |
| Cosine rule | $a^2 = b^2 + c^2 - 2bc\cos A$ | SAS, SSS |
| Area | $\Delta = \tfrac12 bc\sin A$ | two sides + included angle |
| Heron | $\Delta = \sqrt{s(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)}$ | three sides ($s$ = semiperimeter) |
| Circumradius | $R = \dfrac{a}{2\sin A} = \dfrac{abc}{4\Delta}$ | — |
| Inradius | $r = \dfrac{\Delta}{s}$ | — |
| Half-angle | $\sin\frac{A}{2} = \sqrt{\dfrac{(s-b)(s-c)}{bc}}$ | — |
| Euler | $OI^2 = R^2 - 2Rr$ | incentre-to-circumcentre |
| Exradii | $r_1 + r_2 + r_3 = 4R + r$ | — |

## Derivation

Sine rule: drop an altitude from one vertex; $\frac{h}{b} = \sin A$ and $\frac{h}{a} = \sin B$, so $\frac{a}{\sin A} = \frac{b}{\sin B}$; extend to the circumdiameter $2R$. Cosine rule: the law of cosines from the difference of two position vectors, or by dropping an altitude and applying Pythagoras. Heron's formula follows from the $\tfrac12 bc\sin A$ area and the cosine rule. [Full derivations: 03.5-Properties-of-Triangles-Proof]

## Method

1. **Decide the rule:** two angles + a side → sine rule; two sides + included angle or three sides → cosine rule.
2. **SSS:** compute the **largest** angle first (opposite the longest side) — cosine uniquely determines an angle in $(0,\pi)$, sine doesn't.
3. **SSA (ambiguous case):** compute $b\sin A$:
   - $b\sin A > a$ → 0 triangles
   - $b\sin A = a$ → 1 triangle (right)
   - $b\sin A < a < b$ → **2 triangles**
   - $a \ge b$ → 1 triangle
4. **Area:** choose the formula matching the data (sides+included angle, or three sides → Heron).

## Worked Examples

**Setup:** $a=7, b=8, c=9$. Find all angles and area.

**Solution:** $\cos A = \frac{64+81-49}{144} = \frac23 \Rightarrow A \approx 48.19^\circ$; $\cos B = \frac{81+49-64}{126} = \frac{11}{21} \Rightarrow B \approx 58.41^\circ$; $C \approx 73.4^\circ$. $s = 12$, $\Delta = \sqrt{12\cdot5\cdot4\cdot3} = 12\sqrt5$.

**Key insight:** Start with the cosine rule for the largest angle (opposite the longest side).

---

**Setup:** $a=5, b=7, A=30^\circ$. Find all possible triangles.

**Solution:** $b\sin A = 3.5$. Since $3.5 < 5 < 7$, **two** triangles exist. Triangle 1: $B \approx 44.43^\circ$, $C \approx 105.57^\circ$. Triangle 2: $B \approx 135.57^\circ$, $C \approx 14.43^\circ$.

**Key insight:** Always check $b\sin A$ first to determine how many triangles exist.

## Common Traps

- Sine rule silently giving the **ambiguous case** — check $b\sin A$ for SSA data
- $\sin^{-1}$ returning the wrong (supplementary) angle — use the largest-angle-first strategy for SSS
- Applying the sine rule to SAS data (the cosine rule belongs there)
- Forgetting the semiperimeter $s = \frac{a+b+c}{2}$ in Heron
- Rounding too early in multi-step angle computations
