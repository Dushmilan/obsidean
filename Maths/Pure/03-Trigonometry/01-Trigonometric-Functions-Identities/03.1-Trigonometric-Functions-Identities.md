
## Definition

The six trigonometric functions are defined from the unit circle: a point on the circle has coordinates $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$, and the rest follow:

$$\tan\theta = \frac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta},\quad \cot\theta = \frac{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta},\quad \sec\theta = \frac{1}{\cos\theta},\quad \csc\theta = \frac{1}{\sin\theta}$$

Valid when the denominator is nonzero ($\tan$ undefined at $\theta = \frac{\pi}{2} + n\pi$, etc.).

## The Intuition

Walking around a clock face. At each position your distance from the centre is 1 (unit circle), but your horizontal and vertical distances change. **Cosine tracks the left-right shadow on the $x$-axis; sine tracks the up-down shadow on the $y$-axis.** As the point moves, these shadows stretch and shrink between $-1$ and $1$ — which is why $|\sin\theta| \le 1$ and $|\cos\theta| \le 1$.

## The Toolkit

| Result | Formula | Notes |
|--------|---------|-------|
| Pythagorean | $\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1$ | the deepest identity |
| Pythagorean (tan) | $1 + \tan^2\theta = \sec^2\theta$ | divide by $\cos^2$ |
| Pythagorean (cot) | $1 + \cot^2\theta = \csc^2\theta$ | divide by $\sin^2$ |
| Double angle | $\sin 2\theta = 2\sin\theta\cos\theta$ | — |
| Double angle (cos) | $\cos 2\theta = \cos^2\theta - \sin^2\theta = 2\cos^2\theta - 1 = 1 - 2\sin^2\theta$ | three forms |
| Half angle | $\sin\frac{\theta}{2} = \pm\sqrt{\frac{1-\cos\theta}{2}}$ | sign by quadrant |
| Half angle | $\cos\frac{\theta}{2} = \pm\sqrt{\frac{1+\cos\theta}{2}}$ | sign by quadrant |
| Amplitude-phase | $a\sin\theta + b\cos\theta = R\sin(\theta+\alpha)$ | $R = \sqrt{a^2+b^2}$, $\tan\alpha = b/a$ |

## Derivation

The circle equation $x^2 + y^2 = 1$ with $x = \cos\theta$, $y = \sin\theta$ **is** the Pythagorean identity. The tan/cot variants come from dividing by $\cos^2\theta$ or $\sin^2\theta$. Double angles are the compound formulas with $A = B$ (see 03.2-Compound-Multiple-Angles). [Full derivations: 03.1-Trigonometric-Functions-Identities-Proof]

## Method

**Proving an identity:** work one side toward the other —
1. Convert everything to $\sin\theta, \cos\theta$ (quotient/reciprocal rules).
2. Apply the Pythagorean identity to eliminate $1$ or $\sin^2/\cos^2$ pairs.
3. Factor and cancel; multiply by a conjugate when a denominator resists.

**Max/min of $a\sin\theta + b\cos\theta$:** the value oscillates in $[-R, R]$ with $R = \sqrt{a^2+b^2}$ — the max is $R$, the min is $-R$.

## Worked Examples

**Setup:** Prove $\frac{\sin\theta}{1-\cos\theta} = \csc\theta + \cot\theta$.

**Solution:** Multiply by $(1+\cos\theta)$:
$$\frac{\sin\theta(1+\cos\theta)}{1-\cos^2\theta} = \frac{\sin\theta(1+\cos\theta)}{\sin^2\theta} = \frac{1+\cos\theta}{\sin\theta} = \csc\theta + \cot\theta$$

**Key insight:** Multiplying by the conjugate creates a Pythagorean identity in the denominator.

---

**Setup:** Find the maximum of $3\sin\theta + 4\cos\theta$.

**Solution:** $R = \sqrt{9+16} = 5$, so the expression equals $5\sin(\theta+\alpha)$. Maximum $= 5$.

**Key insight:** Any $a\sin\theta + b\cos\theta$ has maximum $\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$.

## Common Traps

- $\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1$ — not $\sin^2 + \cos^2 = \theta$
- $\tan\theta$ undefined where $\cos\theta = 0$; $\sec, \csc$ have their own domains
- Sign of half-angle formulas depends on the quadrant of $\theta/2$ — don't always take $+$
- $a\sin\theta + b\cos\theta$ max is $R$, not $a+b$
- Dividing by a trig function can lose solutions — factor instead
