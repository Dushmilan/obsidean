# Limits & Continuity — Full Derivations

## 1. $\epsilon$-$\delta$ Definition of Limit

**Definition:** $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L$ means:
$$\forall \epsilon > 0,\; \exists \delta > 0 : 0 < |x - a| < \delta \implies |f(x) - L| < \epsilon$$

**Key Points:**
- $\delta$ depends on $\epsilon$ (and possibly $a$)
- $0 < |x - a|$ excludes $x = a$ (limit doesn't require $f(a)$ to exist)
- For one-sided limits: $x \to a^+$ means $0 < x - a < \delta$; $x \to a^-$ means $0 < a - x < \delta$

---

## 2. Limit Laws (Assuming Limits Exist)

Let $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L$, $\lim_{x \to a} g(x) = M$, $c \in \mathbb{R}$.

### Sum/Difference Law
$$\lim_{x \to a} [f(x) \pm g(x)] = L \pm M$$

**Proof:**
Given $\epsilon > 0$, choose $\delta_1$ for $f$ and $\delta_2$ for $g$ with $\epsilon/2$.
Let $\delta = \min(\delta_1, \delta_2)$. Then:
$$|(f(x) \pm g(x)) - (L \pm M)| \le |f(x) - L| + |g(x) - M| < \frac{\epsilon}{2} + \frac{\epsilon}{2} = \epsilon$$

### Product Law
$$\lim_{x \to a} [f(x)g(x)] = LM$$

**Proof:**
$|f(x)g(x) - LM| = |f(x)g(x) - f(x)M + f(x)M - LM| \le |f(x)||g(x) - M| + |M||f(x) - L|$
Since $f(x) \to L$, $|f(x)|$ is bounded near $a$ (say by $B$). Choose $\delta$ so both terms $< \epsilon/2$.

### Quotient Law ($M \neq 0$)
$$\lim_{x \to a} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \frac{L}{M}$$

**Proof:** First show $\lim_{x \to a} 1/g(x) = 1/M$, then use product law.

### Constant Multiple
$$\lim_{x \to a} cf(x) = cL$$

### Power Law
$$\lim_{x \to a} [f(x)]^n = L^n \quad (n \in \mathbb{N})$$

**Proof:** By induction using product law.

## 3. Squeeze (Sandwich) Theorem

**Theorem:** If $f(x) \le g(x) \le h(x)$ for $x$ near $a$ (except possibly at $a$), and
$$\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = \lim_{x \to a} h(x) = L$$
then $\lim_{x \to a} g(x) = L$.

**Proof:**
Given $\epsilon > 0$, $\exists \delta_1, \delta_2$ such that:
$L - \epsilon < f(x) < L + \epsilon$ for $0 < |x-a| < \delta_1$
$L - \epsilon < h(x) < L + \epsilon$ for $0 < |x-a| < \delta_2$
Let $\delta = \min(\delta_1, \delta_2)$. Then $L - \epsilon < f(x) \le g(x) \le h(x) < L + \epsilon$, so $|g(x) - L| < \epsilon$.

## 4. Standard Limits — Proofs

### $\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x}{x} = 1$

**Proof (Geometric):** For $0 < x < \pi/2$:
$$\sin x < x < \tan x \quad \text{(areas: triangle < sector < triangle)}$$
Divide by $\sin x > 0$:
$$1 < \frac{x}{\sin x} < \frac{1}{\cos x} \implies \cos x < \frac{\sin x}{x} < 1$$
As $x \to 0^+$, $\cos x \to 1$. By Squeeze Theorem, $\frac{\sin x}{x} \to 1$.
For $x \to 0^-$, $\frac{\sin(-x)}{-x} = \frac{\sin x}{x} \to 1$.

### $\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1 - \cos x}{x} = 0$

**Proof:**
$$\frac{1 - \cos x}{x} = \frac{2\sin^2(x/2)}{x} = \frac{\sin(x/2)}{x/2} \cdot \sin(x/2) \to 1 \cdot 0 = 0$$

### $\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{e^x - 1}{x} = 1$

**Proof (using series):** $e^x = 1 + x + \frac{x^2}{2!} + \cdots$
$$\frac{e^x - 1}{x} = 1 + \frac{x}{2!} + \frac{x^2}{3!} + \cdots \to 1$$

**Alternative (using $\ln$):** Let $y = e^x - 1 \Rightarrow x = \ln(1+y)$
$$\frac{e^x - 1}{x} = \frac{y}{\ln(1+y)} = \frac{1}{\frac{\ln(1+y)}{y}} \to \frac{1}{1} = 1$$

### $\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\ln(1+x)}{x} = 1$

**Proof:** Let $y = \ln(1+x) \Rightarrow x = e^y - 1$
$$\frac{\ln(1+x)}{x} = \frac{y}{e^y - 1} \to \frac{1}{1} = 1 \quad \text{(by above)}$$

## 5. L'Hôpital's Rule

**Theorem:** If $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = \lim_{x \to a} g(x) = 0$ or $\pm\infty$, and $f,g$ differentiable near $a$ with $g'(x) \neq 0$, and $\lim_{x \to a} \frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}$ exists (or is $\pm\infty$), then
$$\lim_{x \to a} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \lim_{x \to a} \frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}$$

**Proof (for $0/0$ case, $f,g$ differentiable at $a$, $g'(a) \neq 0$):**
$$\frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \frac{f(x) - f(a)}{g(x) - g(a)} = \frac{\frac{f(x)-f(a)}{x-a}}{\frac{g(x)-g(a)}{x-a}} \xrightarrow{x \to a} \frac{f'(a)}{g'(a)}$$

**General Proof (Cauchy Mean Value Theorem):**
For $x \neq a$, apply CMVT on $[a, x]$ (or $[x, a]$):
$$\frac{f(x) - f(a)}{g(x) - g(a)} = \frac{f'(c)}{g'(c)} \quad (c \text{ between } a \text{ and } x)$$
As $x \to a$, $c \to a$, so RHS $\to \lim_{c \to a} \frac{f'(c)}{g'(c)}$.

## 6. Continuity

### Definition at a Point
$f$ is **continuous at $x = a$** iff all three hold:
1. $f(a)$ is defined
2. $\lim_{x \to a} f(x)$ exists
3. $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = f(a)$

### Types of Discontinuity

| Type | Condition | Example |
|------|-----------|---------|
| **Removable** | Limit exists $\neq f(a)$ (or $f(a)$ undefined) | $\frac{\sin x}{x}$ at $0$ |
| **Jump** | Left & right limits exist but differ | $f(x) = \lfloor x \rfloor$ at integers |
| **Infinite** | Limit is $\pm\infty$ | $\frac{1}{x}$ at $0$ |
| **Oscillatory** | No limit (oscillates) | $\sin\frac{1}{x}$ at $0$ |

### Continuity on an Interval
- **Continuous on $(a,b)$:** Continuous at every point in $(a,b)$
- **Continuous on $[a,b]$:** Continuous on $(a,b)$, right-continuous at $a$, left-continuous at $b$

### Properties of Continuous Functions
If $f,g$ continuous at $a$:
- $f \pm g$, $f \cdot g$, $cf$ continuous at $a$
- $f/g$ continuous at $a$ if $g(a) \neq 0$
- $f \circ g$ continuous at $a$ if $g$ continuous at $a$ and $f$ continuous at $g(a)$

**Proof:** Follows directly from limit laws and composition limit theorem.

## 7. Intermediate Value Theorem (IVT)

**Theorem:** If $f$ continuous on $[a,b]$ and $f(a) \neq f(b)$, then for any $N$ between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$, $\exists c \in (a,b)$ such that $f(c) = N$.

**Proof:**
Let $S = \{x \in [a,b] : f(x) \le N\}$. $S$ is non-empty ($a \in S$) and bounded above by $b$.
Let $c = \sup S$. Show $f(c) = N$:
- If $f(c) < N$, by continuity $\exists \delta > 0$ s.t. $f(x) < N$ for $x \in (c, c+\delta)$, contradicting $c = \sup S$.
- If $f(c) > N$, $\exists \delta > 0$ s.t. $f(x) > N$ for $x \in (c-\delta, c]$, contradicting $c$ being least upper bound.

---

## 8. Extreme Value Theorem (EVT)

**Theorem:** If $f$ continuous on closed interval $[a,b]$, then $f$ attains absolute maximum and minimum on $[a,b]$.

**Proof:**
- **Boundedness:** Suppose $f$ unbounded above. Then $\forall n \in \mathbb{N}, \exists x_n \in [a,b]: f(x_n) > n$. By Bolzano-Weierstrass, $(x_n)$ has convergent subsequence $x_{n_k} \to c \in [a,b]$. By continuity, $f(x_{n_k}) \to f(c)$, contradicting $f(x_{n_k}) > n_k \to \infty$.
- **Attainment:** Since $f$ bounded, let $M = \sup_{[a,b]} f(x)$. Suppose $f(x) < M$ for all $x$. Define $g(x) = \frac{1}{M - f(x)}$, continuous on $[a,b]$. Then $g$ is bounded, so $M - f(x) \ge \epsilon > 0$, contradicting $M = \sup$.

## 9. Continuity of Standard Functions

| Function | Domain | Continuous On |
|----------|--------|---------------|
| Polynomials | $\mathbb{R}$ | Everywhere |
| Rational $\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}$ | $\mathbb{R} \setminus \{Q(x)=0\}$ | Domain |
| $\sin x, \cos x$ | $\mathbb{R}$ | Everywhere |
| $\tan x, \sec x$ | $x \neq \frac{\pi}{2} + k\pi$ | Domain |
| $e^x$ | $\mathbb{R}$ | Everywhere |
| $\ln x$ | $(0,\infty)$ | Domain |
| $a^x$ ($a>0$) | $\mathbb{R}$ | Everywhere |
| $\sqrt[n]{x}$ | $x \ge 0$ (even $n$), $\mathbb{R}$ (odd $n$) | Domain |
| $|x|$ | $\mathbb{R}$ | Everywhere (not differentiable at 0) |

## 10. Uniform Continuity

**Definition:** $f$ is uniformly continuous on $A$ if
$$\forall \epsilon > 0,\; \exists \delta > 0 : \forall x,y \in A,\; |x-y| < \delta \implies |f(x)-f(y)| < \epsilon$$
(Note: $\delta$ independent of $x,y$)

**Heine-Cantor Theorem:** If $f$ continuous on **closed bounded interval** $[a,b]$, then $f$ uniformly continuous on $[a,b]$.

**Proof:** Suppose not. Then $\exists \epsilon_0 > 0$ such that $\forall \delta > 0, \exists x_\delta, y_\delta$ with $|x_\delta - y_\delta| < \delta$ but $|f(x_\delta) - f(y_\delta)| \ge \epsilon_0$.
Take $\delta_n = 1/n$, get sequences $x_n, y_n$ with $|x_n - y_n| < 1/n$ and $|f(x_n) - f(y_n)| \ge \epsilon_0$.
By Bolzano-Weierstrass, $x_{n_k} \to c \in [a,b]$. Then $y_{n_k} \to c$ too.
By continuity, $f(x_{n_k}) \to f(c)$ and $f(y_{n_k}) \to f(c)$, so $|f(x_{n_k}) - f(y_{n_k})| \to 0$, contradiction.

## 11. One-Sided Limits & Continuity

- **Right-hand limit:** $\lim_{x \to a^+} f(x) = L \iff \forall \epsilon > 0, \exists \delta > 0 : 0 < x - a < \delta \implies |f(x) - L| < \epsilon$
- **Left-hand limit:** $\lim_{x \to a^-} f(x) = L \iff \forall \epsilon > 0, \exists \delta > 0 : 0 < a - x < \delta \implies |f(x) - L| < \epsilon$

**Theorem:** $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L \iff \lim_{x \to a^+} f(x) = \lim_{x \to a^-} f(x) = L$

**Continuity from right/left:** $f$ continuous from right at $a$ if $\lim_{x \to a^+} f(x) = f(a)$.

---

## 12. Limits at Infinity

**Definition:** $\lim_{x \to \infty} f(x) = L \iff \forall \epsilon > 0, \exists M > 0 : x > M \implies |f(x) - L| < \epsilon$

**Horizontal asymptote:** $y = L$ is a horizontal asymptote if $\lim_{x \to \pm\infty} f(x) = L$

**L'Hôpital at infinity:** If $\lim_{x \to \infty} f(x) = \lim_{x \to \infty} g(x) = 0$ or $\pm\infty$, then $\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}$ (if latter exists).

**Proof:** Substitute $x = 1/t$, let $t \to 0^+$. Then use standard L'Hôpital.

## 13. Indeterminate Forms — Complete Classification

| Form | Resolution |
|------|------------|
| $0/0$ | L'Hôpital, algebraic manipulation, standard limits |
| $\infty/\infty$ | L'Hôpital, divide by highest power |
| $0 \cdot \infty$ | Rewrite as $0/0$ or $\infty/\infty$ |
| $\infty - \infty$ | Combine into single fraction |
| $0^0$, $1^\infty$, $\infty^0$ | Use $y = e^{\ln y}$, then L'Hôpital on exponent |

**Example $1^\infty$:**
$$\lim_{x \to a} f(x)^{g(x)} \text{ where } f \to 1, g \to \infty$$
Let $y = f(x)^{g(x)} \Rightarrow \ln y = g(x)\ln f(x) = \frac{\ln f(x)}{1/g(x)} \to \frac{0}{0}$ form.

## 14. Sequential Criterion for Limits

**Theorem:** $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L \iff$ for every sequence $(x_n)$ with $x_n \neq a$ and $x_n \to a$, we have $f(x_n) \to L$.

**Proof:**
($\Rightarrow$) Given $\epsilon$, choose $\delta$ from limit definition. For $n > N$, $|x_n - a| < \delta$, so $|f(x_n) - L| < \epsilon$.
($\Leftarrow$) Contrapositive: if limit $\neq L$, $\exists \epsilon > 0$ such that $\forall \delta, \exists x_\delta: 0 < |x_\delta - a| < \delta$ but $|f(x_\delta) - L| \ge \epsilon$. Take $\delta_n = 1/n$, get sequence $x_n \to a$ but $f(x_n) \not\to L$.

---

## 15. Continuity of Inverse Functions

**Theorem:** If $f$ is continuous and strictly monotonic on interval $I$, then $f^{-1}$ is continuous on $f(I)$.

**Proof:** Let $y_0 \in f(I)$, $x_0 = f^{-1}(y_0)$. For sequence $y_n \to y_0$, let $x_n = f^{-1}(y_n)$. Since $f$ strictly monotonic, $x_n$ bounded. If $x_n \not\to x_0$, $\exists$ subsequence $x_{n_k} \to c \neq x_0$. By continuity, $f(x_{n_k}) \to f(c) \neq y_0$, contradiction.
