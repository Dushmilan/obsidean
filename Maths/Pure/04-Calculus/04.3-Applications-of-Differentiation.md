Using derivatives to solve real-world and geometric problems: finding best values, rates of change, curve shapes, and approximations. Most calculus problems in physics, engineering, and economics are optimisation or related rates problems — this is where differentiation earns its keep. You can find the tangent and normal to any curve, determine where a function reaches its highest or lowest value, and figure out how quickly one quantity changes when another changes.

**The Intuition:** Imagine you are hiking up a mountain. The derivative tells you the steepness of the ground right where you are standing. If the slope is zero, you are at a peak, a valley, or a flat ridge. Optimisation is finding the highest peak or lowest valley by following the slope information.

**The Math:** Tangent line: $y - y_0 = f'(x_0)(x - x_0)$. Normal line: $y - y_0 = -\frac{1}{f'(x_0)}(x - x_0)$. Rolle's Theorem: $f'(c) = 0$ if $f$ continuous on $[a,b]$, differentiable on $(a,b)$, and $f(a) = f(b)$. Lagrange's MVT: $f'(c) = \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}$. Newton's Method: $x_{n+1} = x_n - \frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}$.

For optimisation: set $f'(x) = 0$ to find critical points, then test with the second derivative ($f''(c) > 0$ means minimum, $f''(c) < 0$ means maximum). On a closed interval $[a,b]$, also check endpoints. For related rates: differentiate the geometric relationship with respect to time $t$, substitute known rates, solve for the unknown rate.

**What does this mean for Pure Mathematics?** Optimisation is perhaps the most important application. Almost every real-world problem that asks for the maximum or minimum of something translates to finding where the derivative equals zero. The mean value theorems connect average and instantaneous rates of change.

**Setup:** A 5m ladder slides down a wall. How fast does the top descend when the bottom is 3m from the wall and moving at 1 m/s? **Solution:** Constraint: $x^2 + y^2 = 25$. Differentiate: $2x\frac{dx}{dt} + 2y\frac{dy}{dt} = 0$. When $x = 3$, $y = 4$: $2(3)(1) + 2(4)\frac{dy}{dt} = 0$, so $\frac{dy}{dt} = -0.75$ m/s. **Key insight:** Differentiate the geometric relationship with respect to time. Substitute known values. Solve for the unknown rate.

**Setup:** Use Newton's method to approximate $\sqrt{2}$. **Solution:** $f(x) = x^2 - 2$, $f'(x) = 2x$. $x_{n+1} = \frac{x_n^2 + 2}{2x_n}$. Start with $x_0 = 1$: $x_1 = 1.5$, $x_2 \approx 1.4167$, $x_3 \approx 1.4142$. **Key insight:** Newton's method converges very fast but requires a good initial guess and $f'(x) \neq 0$.
