Multiple integrals extend single-variable integration to functions of two or more variables, computing volumes, masses, and other quantities over regions in 2D or 3D. A single integral sums $f(x)$ along a line. A double integral sums $f(x, y)$ over a region in the plane — computing the volume under a surface $z = f(x, y)$. A triple integral sums $f(x, y, z)$ over a solid in space.

**The Intuition:** A double integral is like computing the volume of a building by dividing the floor into tiny squares and multiplying each square's area by the building's height at that point. Sum all the tiny volumes and you get the total. A triple integral does the same in 3D — divide space into tiny cubes and sum.

**The Math:** Double integral (Type I): $\int_a^b \int_{g_1(x)}^{g_2(x)} f(x, y)\,dy\,dx$. Polar coordinates: $\int \int f(r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta)\,r\,dr\,d\theta$ (note the extra $r$ from the Jacobian). Triple integral in spherical coordinates: $\int \int \int f(\rho, \theta, \phi)\,\rho^2 \sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta$.

Coordinate transformations: polar ($x = r\cos\theta$, $y = r\sin\theta$, Jacobian $= r$), cylindrical (Jacobian $= r$), spherical ($x = \rho\sin\phi\cos\theta$, Jacobian $= \rho^2\sin\phi$). Change of variables: $\int_R f(x, y)\,dx\,dy = \int_S f(x(u,v), y(u,v))\,|J|\,du\,dv$.

**What does this mean for Pure Mathematics?** The key technique is converting to iterated integrals: integrate with respect to one variable first, then the other. Fubini's theorem guarantees this works for continuous functions. Coordinate transformations simplify integrals over regions with symmetry.

**Setup:** Evaluate $\int_R (x + y)\,dA$ where $R$ is bounded by $y = x^2$ and $y = 2x$. **Solution:** Intersections: $x = 0, 2$. Type I: $0 \leq x \leq 2$, $x^2 \leq y \leq 2x$. $\int_0^2 \int_{x^2}^{2x} (x + y)\,dy\,dx = \int_0^2 (4x^2 - x^3 - x^4/2)\,dx = \frac{16}{5}$. **Key insight:** Set up the region first. Determine limits from the geometry. Integrate inner first, then outer.

**Setup:** Evaluate $\int_0^1 \int_x^1 \sin(y^2)\,dy\,dx$. **Solution:** Original order impossible ($\sin(y^2)$ has no elementary antiderivative). Switch order: $\int_0^1 \int_0^y \sin(y^2)\,dx\,dy = \int_0^1 y\sin(y^2)\,dy = \frac{1 - \cos 1}{2}$. **Key insight:** If one order of integration is impossible, try switching. Sketch the region to find new limits.
