# First-Order Linear Differential Equations

A first-order linear equation has the form
$$\frac{dy}{dx} + P(x)\,y = Q(x)$$
and the **integrating factor** $\mu = e^{\int P\,dx}$ turns the left side into a single exact derivative, reducing the whole equation to one integration.

**The Intuition:** You can't directly integrate $y' + P(x)y = Q(x)$ because the left side isn't a plain derivative — yet. The integrating factor is a *multiplier* chosen so that after multiplying through, the left side becomes exactly $\frac{d}{dx}\big[\mu y\big]$. Once it's a perfect derivative, solving is just "integrate both sides."

## The method

$$\frac{dy}{dx} + P(x)y = Q(x) \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \mu = e^{\int P\,dx}$$

1. Put the equation in standard form: $\frac{dy}{dx} + P(x)y = Q(x)$.
2. Compute $\mu = e^{\int P\,dx}$ (constant of integration unnecessary — absorbed).
3. Multiply through: $\mu y' + \mu P y = \mu Q$, i.e. $\frac{d}{dx}[\mu y] = \mu Q$.
4. Integrate: $\mu y = \int \mu Q\,dx + C$.
5. Solve for $y$: $y = \frac{1}{\mu}\Big(\int \mu Q\,dx + C\Big)$.

**Why it works:** $\frac{d}{dx}[\mu y] = \mu y' + \mu' y$ and $\mu' = P\mu$, so $\frac{d}{dx}[\mu y] = \mu y' + \mu P y$ — exactly the left side after multiplication.

## The first-order initial-value problem

An initial condition $y(x_0) = y_0$ pins down the constant:

$$y(x) = e^{-\int_{x_0}^x P\,dt}\bigg(y_0 + \int_{x_0}^x Q(t)\,e^{\int_{x_0}^t P\,ds}dt\bigg)$$

In practice: solve the general solution, then substitute the condition to find $C$.

---

**Setup:** Solve $\frac{dy}{dx} + 2xy = x$.

**Solution:** $P(x) = 2x$, so $\mu = e^{\int 2x\,dx} = e^{x^2}$. Multiply:
$$\frac{d}{dx}\big[y e^{x^2}\big] = x e^{x^2}$$
Integrate: $y e^{x^2} = \frac{1}{2}e^{x^2} + C$, so
$$y = \frac{1}{2} + Ce^{-x^2}$$

**Key insight:** the integrating factor converts "solve a differential equation" into "recognize a product rule." Everything after that is ordinary integration.

---

**Setup:** Solve $\frac{dy}{dx} + y = x$ with $y(0) = 1$.

**Solution:** $\mu = e^{\int 1\,dx} = e^x$. Then $\frac{d}{dx}[ye^x] = xe^x$. Integrate by parts: $ye^x = (x-1)e^x + C$, so $y = x - 1 + Ce^{-x}$. Using $y(0)=1$: $1 = -1 + C \Rightarrow C = 2$. Hence
$$y = x - 1 + 2e^{-x}$$

**Key insight:** the transient term $Ce^{-x}$ decays to zero; the steady solution $x - 1$ remains. The initial condition selects which transient.

---

**Setup:** Why can we drop the constant when computing $\mu = e^{\int P\,dx}$?

**Solution:** An added constant in $\int P\,dx$ becomes a constant *factor* in $\mu$ — and since we multiply both sides of the equation by $\mu$, any constant factor cancels. $\mu$ is only needed up to a nonzero scalar.

**Key insight:** the integrating factor is defined "up to a constant multiple." Using $e^{\int P\,dx + C} = e^C e^{\int P\,dx}$ changes nothing — the $e^C$ multiplies both sides.

## Practice (try before peeking)

1. What is $\mu$ for $\frac{dy}{dx} + 3y = e^{2x}$?
2. After multiplying by $\mu$, what does the left side always become?
3. A tank fills and drains at rates such that $Q' + \frac{1}{10}Q = 5$ — what shape is the steady solution?

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. $\mu = e^{\int 3\,dx} = e^{3x}$.
2. $\frac{d}{dx}[\mu y]$ — the product rule in disguise. That's the entire trick.
3. Steady state: $Q' \to 0$, so $\frac{1}{10}Q = 5 \Rightarrow Q = 50$. The full solution is $Q = 50 + Ce^{-t/10}$ — a constant plus a decaying exponential.

</details>

---

**Common traps:**
- Forgetting to put the equation in standard form first (coefficient of $y'$ must be 1)
- Dropping the absolute value in $\int \frac{1}{x}\,dx = \ln|x|$ when it matters
- Missing the constant of integration on the right side
- Applying the method to nonlinear equations — it only works when the equation is linear in $y$
