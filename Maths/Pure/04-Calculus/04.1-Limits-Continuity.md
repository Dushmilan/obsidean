Limits describe what a function approaches as the input approaches a value, and continuity means the function has no breaks, jumps, or holes at that point. Every derivative and integral in calculus is built on the idea of a limit — without limits, calculus does not exist. Before Newton and Leibniz formalised differentiation and integration, they needed a way to talk about instantaneous change, and limits provide exactly that.

**The Intuition:** Think of a limit as guessing what number a function is heading towards without ever actually arriving there. Like walking towards a wall — you get infinitely close but never touch it, yet you can clearly predict where you are heading. Continuity means the graph does not suddenly jump to a different height or vanish at that exact point.

**The Math:** $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L$ means $f(x)$ can be made arbitrarily close to $L$ by taking $x$ sufficiently close to $a$ (but $x \neq a$). The formal $\varepsilon$-$\delta$ definition: for all $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists $\delta > 0$ such that $0 < |x - a| < \delta \implies |f(x) - L| < \varepsilon$.

One-sided limits: $\lim_{x \to a^+} f(x) = L$ (right-hand) and $\lim_{x \to a^-} f(x) = L$ (left-hand). The two-sided limit exists iff both one-sided limits exist and are equal. Continuity at $x = a$ requires three conditions: $f(a)$ is defined, $\lim_{x \to a} f(x)$ exists, and $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = f(a)$.

Key standard limits: $\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x}{x} = 1$, $\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{e^x - 1}{x} = 1$, $\lim_{x \to \infty} (1 + 1/x)^x = e$. L'Hôpital's Rule: if $\lim f(x)/g(x)$ gives $0/0$ or $\infty/\infty$, then $\lim \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \lim \frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}$ provided the right side exists.

**What does this mean for Pure Mathematics?** Limits are the bedrock upon which differentiation and integration are built. Master these and the rest of calculus flows naturally. The $\varepsilon$-$\delta$ definition is what separates rigorous mathematics from hand-waving.

**Setup:** Evaluate $\lim_{x \to 2} \frac{x^3 - 8}{x^2 - 4}$. **Solution:** Factorise: $\frac{(x-2)(x^2+2x+4)}{(x-2)(x+2)} = \frac{x^2+2x+4}{x+2}$. Substitute $x = 2$: $\frac{4+4+4}{4} = 3$. **Key insight:** Always try factorisation first — it avoids L'Hôpital entirely.

**Setup:** Evaluate $\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin(5x)}{\sin(2x)}$. **Solution:** Rewrite: $\frac{5x}{2x} \cdot \frac{\sin(5x)}{5x} \cdot \frac{2x}{\sin(2x)} = \frac{5}{2} \cdot 1 \cdot 1 = \frac{5}{2}$. **Key insight:** Manipulate to isolate known standard limits.

**Setup:** Evaluate $\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{e^{2x} - 1 - 2x}{x^2}$. **Solution:** Form is $0/0$. Apply L'Hôpital: $\lim \frac{2e^{2x} - 2}{2x}$. Still $0/0$. Apply again: $\lim \frac{4e^{2x}}{2} = 2$. **Key insight:** You can apply L'Hôpital repeatedly as long as the form remains indeterminate.
