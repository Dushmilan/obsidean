Partial differentiation extends derivatives to functions of multiple variables — you differentiate with respect to one variable while holding the others constant. Most real-world functions depend on multiple inputs (temperature depends on position, profit depends on price and quantity). Partial derivatives describe how the function changes as each input changes independently.

**The Intuition:** Imagine standing on a hillside. The partial derivative with respect to $x$ tells you the slope if you walk east (keeping $y$ fixed). The partial derivative with respect to $y$ tells you the slope if you walk north (keeping $x$ fixed). The gradient combines these into the direction of steepest ascent.

**The Math:** $f_x = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h, y) - f(x, y)}{h}$. Chain rule: if $z = f(x, y)$, $x = x(t)$, $y = y(t)$, then $\frac{dz}{dt} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\frac{dx}{dt} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\frac{dy}{dt}$. Gradient: $\nabla f = (f_x, f_y)$. Directional derivative: $D_{\mathbf{u}} f = \nabla f \cdot \mathbf{u}$.

Tangent plane to $z = f(x, y)$ at $(x_0, y_0)$: $z - z_0 = f_x(x_0, y_0)(x - x_0) + f_y(x_0, y_0)(y - y_0)$. Second derivative test: $D = f_{xx} f_{yy} - (f_{xy})^2$. $D > 0$, $f_{xx} > 0$: local minimum. $D > 0$, $f_{xx} < 0$: local maximum. $D < 0$: saddle point.

Lagrange multipliers: max/min $f(x, y)$ subject to $g(x, y) = c$: $\nabla f = \lambda \nabla g$ and $g = c$.

**What does this mean for Pure Mathematics?** The gradient vector collects all partial derivatives into a single vector pointing in the direction of steepest ascent. The chain rule extends naturally to multivariable functions, but you must account for all paths through which a variable can change.

**Setup:** Find $f_x$, $f_y$, $f_{xy}$ for $f(x,y) = x^2 y + \sin(xy)$. **Solution:** $f_x = 2xy + y\cos(xy)$, $f_y = x^2 + x\cos(xy)$, $f_{xy} = 2x + \cos(xy) - xy\sin(xy)$. **Key insight:** Treat the other variable as a constant. For mixed partials, differentiate the first partial with respect to the other variable.

**Setup:** Maximise $f = xy$ subject to $x^2 + y^2 = 1$. **Solution:** $\nabla f = (y, x)$, $\nabla g = (2x, 2y)$. $y = 2\lambda x$, $x = 2\lambda y \Rightarrow x^2 = y^2$. Constraint: $2x^2 = 1 \Rightarrow x = \pm 1/\sqrt{2}$. Max $f = 1/2$. **Key insight:** Solve $\nabla f = \lambda \nabla g$ together with $g = c$. The $\lambda$ is a byproduct — you usually do not need its value.
